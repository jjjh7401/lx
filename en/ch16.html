Chapter 16

Cue Sheets and the Beat Grid

Turning a song into a lighting script

In this chapter

  • Check the finished cue sheet with the fourteen self-checks.
  • Sort the LOVE ATTACK cue sheet into keep, fix and change according to the results, and correct it.
Opening scene

“About how many cues go into one song?” It is a question people often ask when they first learn lighting. The answer differs from person to person. One director writes more than 60 cues for a single three-minute song. Another show gets by with one look per section and just ten or so cues per song. Both can be good shows.

The number of cues is only a result. A good cue sheet is judged not by the number but by whether someone else can read it. When the director says at rehearsal, “Chorus 2 entry,” can you find that cue right away? Can a different operator run the same show? In this chapter, the last of Part 3, you gather what you decided in Chapters 11–15 onto a single cue sheet.

16.1 The Cue List Format for One Song

Make one cue list for each song, and number the cues in section order.

If you number the whole show 1, 2, 3 and so on, every number shifts whenever you remove a song or change the order. If you number each song separately, the numbers stay as they are when you move a song.

99 Finish A cue for the bow, or a transition cue that hands over to the next song (Chapter 14)

There are five rules.

  • One whole number per sectionGive each section one number in multiples of 10. When you insert a cue later, use a decimal (30.5).
  • Start repeated sections from a copyVerse 2 copies the cues of Verse 1 and adds only one new element (the round-by-round build-up of Chapter 14). If you build it from scratch, the relationship between rounds is lost.
  • Name by section and jobWrite the section name and what the cue does, as in “30 Chorus 1 · Brightness hit.” It is easier to find if it matches the name people use at rehearsal.
  • The key stays off the listDo not put the key in the song’s cue list; keep it separate (Chapter 13).
  • A cue to go back toSet up in advance a base cue you can call at once when you get lost, and a cue that stops the stage in one go (freeze, all key).
One number

In the cases collected in the author’s notes, a simple show like a DJ stage had around 5 cues per song, a one-off broadcast stage around 10, and a big tour several hundred.1 The LOVE ATTACK cue sheet in this book has 32 cues in the scene sequence (including blocking and finish). More important than the number is the interval at which you press GO. The places you press by hand are usually every 4–8 bars, not every beat. What happens on each beat is left to effects and rhythm layers (Chapter 13). Cues that are hard for a hand to follow, such as a hit a few beats after a dark cue (29 → 30, 79 → 80) or cues a bar apart (60 → 60.1), are set to go automatically after a time set in the previous cue (a Time trigger, Chapter 27) or locked to timecode.

16.2 The Columns of a Cue Sheet

One line of a cue sheet records when that cue starts and how, how long it takes, and what it does.

The console’s cue list shows only the number, name and time, but a paper cue sheet also records the bar, the clock time and even the layers that cue turns on and off. Even if the operator changes at rehearsal, this one sheet should let them run the same show.

16.3 Fourteen Self-Checks

When you have written the cue sheet, check with fourteen items, before rehearsal, whether you kept the principles set in Part 3.

This check does not block you; it lets you know. If an item is flagged but you meant it, leave it as it is. Just write in the memo column that it was intended.

Table 16-3 Fourteen self-checks 1
No. Check Where to look
1 At least one cue per section. If none, write “keep as is” Cue column
2 The key level is kept while energy rises Key
3 Brightness, effect speed and beam width are not all at maximum together before the peak Scene column (headroom, Chapter 10)
4 Beam width is kept or widened at boundaries where energy rises Scene column
5 Four colors or fewer in the palette (main 1, sub 1–2, special 0–1; white and key color temperature excluded) Palette (Chapter 8)

7 The back is brighter than the key only for a silhouette Scene column
8 No more than two blackouts that turn everything off, key included (Chapter 10) Scene column
9 Snaps only at the song start, hits, drop or beat-section entry, and a button ending In time column (Chapter 14)
10 The number of effects running at once (rhythm layers) is within the energy budget Layers column (Table 12-4 in Chapter 12)
11 Effect cycles are 1, 2, 4 or 8 beats Layers column (Chapter 12)
12 One or two attributes change greatly between neighboring cues In time column (Chapter 14)
13 The same position, the same two colors and the same effect do not carry over into neighboring sections Scene column (Chapter 9)

Follow along

  3. From the Chapter 14 transition table, move the in time and dark cue (including breaks) for each boundary.

  5. Calculate the time from the bar and beat and write it in.

How to check
  1. With the finished cue sheet in hand, play the song from start to finish and, watching the Time column, pretend to press GO by hand.

16.5 Keep, Fix, Change

An item that was flagged is handled in one of three ways: leave it as it is, fix only that cue, or go back and fix the design itself.

Keep means it was intended, so you leave it and write the reason; fix means correcting the values of a single cue; change means going back to the design in another chapter and correcting it there. Sorting into the three shows at once how much there is to fix before rehearsal.

Try it with your own song
  1. Move the cue sheet of the song you are working on now into the columns of Table 16-4, and check it with the fourteen items.
  2. Sort the flagged items into keep, fix and change and count them.
  3. If changes exceed three, it may be faster to look again at the Part 2 design (concept, palette, looks) from the start.

Key points

  • Keep one cue list per song, with whole numbers in multiples of 10 for sections and decimals for inserted cues.
  • Repeated sections copy the previous round and add one new element. Name cues by section and job, and keep the key off the list.
  • The interval at which you press GO matters more than the number of cues. Press every 4–8 bars, and leave what happens on each beat to effects and rhythm layers.
  • When the cue sheet is written, check it with the fourteen self-checks. The check does not block; it lets you know.
  • Sort flagged items into keep, fix and change. Do changes first, and write the reason for keeps.

Closing Part 3

In Part 3 you laid the design set in Part 2 onto beats and bars. You decided the musical unit each attribute follows (Chapter 11), chose starting values for each section (Chapter 12), split rhythm layers into sequences (Chapter 13), refined the transitions between sections (Chapter 14), adjusted for genre and venue (Chapter 15), and finally gathered it all onto one cue sheet (Chapter 16).

This cue sheet is still a script on paper. Even the same script changes in a music show studio, at a tour venue and on an awards stage. Part 4 covers how to rewrite this script for each venue.
