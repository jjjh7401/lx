Directing the Song

Once you have a rough picture, decide what to change and when, in the order the song unfolds. Finally you adjust for genre and gather everything on one cue sheet.

Chapter 11

Principles of Placement

How fast intensity, direction and color change

In this chapter
  • Decide which musical unit each of dimmer, color, position and movement follows.
  • Keep the scene from getting busy by limiting the number of rhythm layers and how often the solo changes.
  • Build a beat grid of LOVE ATTACK bars 1–25, divided into roles and four-bar cells.
Opening scene

You play a song start to finish with one cue hung on each section. The cues go in on time, and the color changes with every section. Nothing is wrong, yet the stage does not follow the music. For the whole twenty seconds of the verse, the same picture just stands there.

So you chop the cues finely, one per beat. Now the stage turns frantic. The color changes on every beat so no color stays, and the moving lights nod in place.

In both cases the problem was not how finely you cut. It was what you change, at which unit. The concept, palette, looks and main light you chose in Part 2 now go onto beats and bars. This chapter, the first of Part 3, covers the principles of that placement.

11.1 Attributes and Musical Units

Each thing a light can change, such as brightness, color and direction, has its own fitting speed. Brightness can change on every beat, but color has to change over a few bars before the eye takes it in.

In Chapter 6 you saw the five attributes a light can change (dimmer, color, position, beam and time), and in Chapter 2 you saw that the time of music is layered as beats, bars, four-bar groups and sections. One rule pairs them. Give an attribute the eye notices quickly to a short unit, and an attribute that reads slowly to a long unit.

Table 11-1 Attributes and musical units — based on LOVE ATTACK (112 BPM) Starting value
Musical unit Length Calculation Attribute it takes Example
Beat 0.54 s Dimmer A back pulse on the kick, a side chase that lights left and right alternately on every beat
Bar 2.1 s Position On beat 1 of the bar, the movers go to a new position
2–4 bars 4.3–8.6 s Shape of movement, color tint From a tilt wave to a pan wave, from pearl pink bleeding toward lilac
Section 15–30 s Palette, base level, beam width From the verse’s pearl pink and lilac to the chorus’s aqua

The dimmer is noticed fastest by the eye, so it takes the beat. Position has to wait for the fixture to physically turn, so it takes the bar. Color takes time before a changed color is accepted as the color of the scene, so it takes 2–4 bars and the section (starting values drawn from the author’s experience on site). This pairing places on one song what you saw as a starting value in Table 6-1 of Chapter 6.

A short experiment
  1. Play a song you like, snap your fingers on every beat, and stamp your foot on the first beat of each bar.
  2. The fingers keep up easily, but the foot misses a few times.
  3. Nodding your head on every fourth bar is harder still.
  4. The eye works the same way.
  5. Small changes follow the beat, while big changes read only over long units.

11.2 Position and Color Do Not Change on Every Beat

Move position only on the first beat of the bar (the downbeat) and on drum fills, and change a scene’s color at section changes or every four bars (Starting value). Change them on every beat and neither the fixtures nor the eye can keep up.

If you give movers a new position on every beat, the next position arrives before the fixture does. Then the movers cannot swing wide and nod in place instead. If you change color on every beat, the eye cannot read the color and sees only flicker.

Before and after the fix

Before One version of the light show script I made called the movers’ position again on every hit. That was 14 times in 2.5 bars, with a median interval of 0.33 s. The movers nodded without finishing the shape they were drawing.1

After Position was called once on the first beat of the section and then left alone. The interval to the next position grew to 1.8–8 s, and in between the movers drew their shapes to the end with an effect.1

If you really need color that rides the beat, do not change the whole scene’s color. Give it to one narrow fixture group, such as a pixel bar, as a two-color snap effect (Chapter 8). Count this not as a change in scene color but as one rhythm layer added (Section 11.3).

The exception is the biggest moment of the song. At a big hit such as a chorus entry, you can cut the color in one go. That moment looks special because you do not usually cut colors.

Heard in the field

“Make it a bit flashier here.” When you feel like changing color on every beat, first work out whether that line means “I want something moving on every beat.” Usually one dimmer pulse is enough.

11.3 The Number of Rhythm Layers

In this book, whatever moves to the tempo on top of a scene is called a rhythm layer. Keep the rhythm layers running at the same time in one scene to two at most (Starting value).

Rhythm layers come in three kinds.

  • PulseA layer where the dimmer jumps to the beat. It is mostly handled by the back or floor beams.
  • ChaseA layer where dimmer or color travels from fixture to fixture. The sides or the pixel bars handle it.
  • MovingA layer where moving lights draw shapes with pan and tilt. If two mover groups are merged into the same movement, count them as one.

With four or five layers, the audience cannot follow any of them. If you keep layers at two as usual, the moment it becomes three feels like the peak of the song. The number of layers is also a value to save, like the headroom of Chapter 10. In LOVE ATTACK, the only place with three layers is Chorus 3.

Let me make one term clear here. Layers pile up, but the means of entry changes. When a chorus repeats, brightness and layers can be added with each round. But the means that announces the start of the chorus (a brightness hit, a mover burst, a white flash) is not stacked on the previous round’s; you switch to a different one. It is the same rule as the chorus build-up in Chapter 3.

11.4 How Often the Solo Changes

Within the main group, the group that moves the most is called the solo. Change the solo every four bars, and do not let the same movement run past eight bars (Starting value).1

In Chapter 9 you chose one role per scene as the main, the one that looks biggest. But in a 15-bar section like the chorus, if the main keeps doing the same movement, the audience stops watching it around the fourth bar. So you leave the main as it is and change the group stepping forward inside it every four bars. It is the solo of Chapter 9 used in short four-bar units. The main of the LOVE ATTACK chorus is the beams, so the beam movers on the floor and the spot movers on the front truss trade the solo every four bars.

In a section where the key is the main, such as the verse, you do not put in a solo. If the movers step forward while the singer sings, the eye leaves the singing (Chapter 9). Then the movers move only as a slow background layer.

The number four is not a measured industry standard. Pop song phrases usually group into four or eight bars (Section 2.3 of Chapter 2), so I settled on this unit as a default after trying it on real songs. If a song groups its phrases differently, follow that song’s phrases.

11.5 Trading Between Upper and Lower Movers

If the moving lights are split into two groups, such as the upper truss and the floor, do not move them like one body. When one side moves, the other takes over, and they alternate as if trading words.

When the upper movers call, the lower movers answer with a different shape on a different axis. It is the call and response of music, where one instrument calls and another answers, moved onto two mover groups.

The exception is the build-up. In the pre-chorus, upper and lower speed up together in the same movement. Just by two groups that usually move separately becoming one, tension appears. They have to be divided usually for merging to have force.

In the verse, you keep one mover group still. As you saw in Chapter 9, movement shows best next to something that is still. If every mover is spinning from the start of the song, there is no more movement to release in the chorus. The starting value for the verse is one slow, the other still, alternating every four bars.

11.6 A Dark Cue One Bar Before the Hit

Just before a big hit, empty the stage briefly, within one bar, and then burst it open exactly on the first beat (the downbeat) of the next bar. The hit looks big only if there was time left empty.

Where the music empties (break, kick-out), bring the scene down to the base only and stop the movement. In that time, you prepare the next color and position and burst them on the downbeat. This is what the dark cue of Chapter 10 does inside a song.

In LOVE ATTACK, bar 17 empties beats 3 and 4. Then on beat 1 of bar 18, Chorus 1 comes in as a brightness hit. Without the two empty beats, bar 18 would just be a moment of getting brighter. Bar 67 is the same. While the music empties beats 1–3, the lights also drop to the base only, and on the anticipation of beat 4, a white flash opens Chorus 3.

This chapter’s principles work together with Part 2’s. Keep the key on until the song ends (Chapter 6), use pastels wide and primary colors short (Chapter 8), and decide where to bring out the special cues for the first time (Chapter 10). Even in a dark cue, you do not turn the key off. The key stays apart from the song cues (Chapter 13).

11.7 When Sequences Overlap (HTP and LTP)

Leave color and position to one sequence per group (a sequence is a list that stores cues in order and plays them). If two sequences send different values to the same fixture, which value shows is decided by the console’s rules.

If you run several layers as separate sequences (Chapter 13), such moments come up often.

Terms to know — HTP and LTP
HTP (Highest Takes Precedence)
The higher of several values wins. Many consoles use this for the dimmer. If the scene holds the back at 40% and a pulse sends 100%, 100% is what you see.
LTP (Latest Takes Precedence)
The value sent later wins. Color and position usually work this way. If two sequences send a position to the same mover, only the one sent last remains.
On grandMA3
You pick a priority per sequence. Set to HTP, that sequence’s dimmer takes the higher value and its other attributes are handled as LTP.2 Which setting is the default differs by console and version, so we come back to it in Part 5.

So if the scene sequence holds the movers’ base position, the movement sequence adds only relative values on top of it.

Heard in the field

“I added a flicker and I can’t see it.” In a setting where the dimmer is handled as HTP, if the scene cue is already holding that fixture’s dimmer at 100%, a dimmer pulse added on top will not show, because the higher value wins. In a setting handled as LTP, whichever ran later wins. Either way, it is solved by leaving the dimmer of the fixture that carries the pulse to one sequence only (Chapter 13).

11.8 [Practice] A Beat Grid for LOVE ATTACK Bars 1–25

Gather this chapter’s principles in one table. This book calls that table a beat grid.

The rows are roles, and the cells are pieces of a section about four bars long. In each cell you write what that role does. An empty cell means it rests.

The fixtures are the practice fixtures of Table 6-4 in Chapter 6. The upper movers are the front-truss spot movers, the lower movers are the beam movers on the floor and towers, the back is the group among the upper-truss wash movers that handles the back, the side is the wash movers on the side towers, and the pixel is the LED bars. The scene’s looks, colors and levels are taken as they are from what Part 2 decided (Chapters 8–10).

Follow along

  1. Copy the section boundaries of bars 1–25 from the song structure table, and divide each section into cells of about four bars.
  2. On the “Scene” row, write for each section the look from Chapter 9 and the base level from Chapter 10. Also write the bar where the key comes on.
  3. Copy the changes you marked in Chapter 4 (entries, build-ups, big hits) to the “Accent” row, and write the beats to empty in the cell one bar before the hit.
  4. For each cell, pick two or fewer layers to ride the rhythm. Write pulse as beat, position as the first beat of the bar, and color tint as four bars.
  5. In each cell where the main is not the key, mark one solo with ★. Check that the same solo does not pass eight bars.
  6. Check whether the upper and lower movers do the same movement in the same cell. Unless it is a pre-chorus, hold one still or switch it to a different axis.
How to check
  1. Read the filled beat grid vertically, cell by cell.
  2. If there is a cell with three or more rhythm layers, a cell where position changes on every beat, or a cell where the same solo runs past eight bars, mark it and write beside it whether that was intended or a slip.
  3. Compare with the author’s one example (Table 11-2).
  4. It is something to compare against, not the right answer.

Key points

  • Each attribute follows a different musical unit: dimmer on the beat, position on the bar, movement and color tint on 2–4 bars, and palette on the section.
  • Do not change position or a scene’s color on every beat. Color that rides the beat goes to one layer on a narrow fixture group such as a pixel bar.
  • Rhythm layers are usually two, and three only at the peak of the song. Layers pile up, but the means of entry changes.
  • Change the solo within the main group every four bars, and do not let the same movement pass eight bars. Put no solo in a verse where the key is the main.
  • Two mover groups trade in call and response, and merge only in a build-up. In a verse, hold one still.
  • Empty a big hit with a dark cue within one bar, and strike on the downbeat.
  • The dimmer is often higher-value-wins (HTP), while color and position are often later-value-wins (LTP). Leave color and position to one sequence per group.
