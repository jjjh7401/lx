Lighting by Show Type

Even for the same song, what you must look after first depends on the stage you stand on. On a music show it is the picture on camera; at a concert, the flow of the whole show; at an awards show, the order and what lies between segments; in a light show, the light itself; at a festival, the quickness to respond on the spot. The last chapter covers choosing fixtures when you design a stage yourself and organizing them into a plot and patch sheet.

Chapter 17

TV Music Shows

A stage where the camera is the audience

In this chapter
  • Learn the week of a music show’s lighting, and the order for preparing one song in a short time.
  • Check the four camera standards: a fixed key, faces that wash out to white, the LED wall, and flicker.
  • Convert the broadcast flash standard into the song’s BPM, and move LOVE ATTACK onto a music show stage.
Opening scene

If you have watched a music show on television and then gone to that studio in person, you will have had a strange experience. The stage that looked crisp and bright on screen looks flatter and brighter than you expected from the seats. A strong light is fixed on the face from the front, and the moving lights look less showy than they did on screen.

It is not strange. The real audience of that stage is not the seats but the camera. The viewers in front of screens far outnumber the few hundred in the seats. Music show lighting is built around the picture the camera sees.

Up to Part 3 you learned how to direct a single song in depth. Part 4 is about how the same song changes from one show type to the next. The first is the music show. The time is the shortest and the standards the strictest.

17.1 A Week of Music Show Lighting

Music show lighting runs in weekly cycles, and the time to prepare one song is always tight.

At the start of the week the set design arrives, then the production designer, camera director and lighting director meet, and the lighting is rigged until the early morning of the broadcast day. With many songs on the bill, there is not much time to prepare lighting for each.1

Table 17-1 A week of music show lighting — a typical flow 1 [Author to confirm]
When What happens
Monday The set design arrives
Tuesday Art, camera and lighting meeting
Before the broadcast Prepare lighting for each song on the bill (time is tight for each)
Early morning of broadcast day Finish the rig
Broadcast day Fix things at the camera rehearsal, then go live

There are two things to read from this flow. Time to prepare each song is tight, and the chances to fix things are one or two rehearsals (the number of rehearsals is from the author’s field experience).[Author to confirm] You cannot refine one song over several days, as you can for a concert. So what music show lighting needs is a compressed order of the process in Parts 1–3 (Section 17.3).

17.2 Limited Fixtures, a Different Song Every Week

On a music show you have to get through many songs of different genres with the fixtures hung on that day’s stage.

Stage design and fixture lists differ by broadcaster and period, and may change from one broadcast to the next. But within one broadcast you cannot pick the kind of fixture for each song on the bill. A dance song, then a ballad, then a band all step onto the same stage. The task of music show lighting is to change the stage in an instant to music of a different genre.2

So on a music show one fixture plays several roles. The same mover is a soft spot with a gobo in a ballad and a beam in a dance song. You change the character of the song not with the kind of fixture but with the cue.

Table 17-2 Roles of music show fixtures — starting values seen through the roles of Chapter 6 [Author to confirm]
Role Fixtures and position mainly used What changes from song to song
Key Follow spots behind the audience, profiles on the front truss Does not change. Color temperature and level fixed
Back Wash movers on the rear truss and floor The song’s color
Wash/spot dual use Overhead movers Change the genre with gobo, zoom and color temperature
Beam Behind the stage, at the sides, on the floor Takes the beat in fast songs
FX Strobe, blinder Only within the broadcast standard (Section 17.5)
Pixel LED bars, set corners Matched to the video and color
Audience Over the seats Audience reaction shots

What to notice in this table is that the key is separate. However many moving lights there are, the key that lights the face is run separately. Whatever song changes or effects run, the key does not move. The principle of Chapter 13, “keep the key outside the song’s sequences,” becomes stricter on a music show.

If the lighting director designs this fixture list directly, the roles needed in common across many songs are the baseline, and fixtures needed for only one song are added for that stage. The order for settling fixtures, the lighting plot and the patch sheet from a song’s looks is covered in Chapter 22.

17.3 Preparing a Song in a Short Time

If you cut the process of Parts 1–3 down to seven steps, you can prepare even a song you have just been given in a short time. Done in order, it saves time; skip a step and you end up doing it again at rehearsal.

Table 17-3 Preparing a song in a short time — starting values
Order What to do What to settle Where in the book
1 Watch the music video and the stage video One or two colors of the artist’s concept, the places where the choreography freezes Chapter 7
2 Decide whether to follow the fast beat or the slow beat The basis for the rhythm layers. For a half-time song, the slow side Chapters 2 and 12
3 Fill in the song structure table while listening Sections, rounds, places of big hits Chapters 3, 4 and 5
4 Decide the palette Main and sub colors; white only for accents Chapter 8
5 Pick one look for each section A cue on the downbeat of each section boundary Chapters 9 and 12
6 Place accents where the choreography freezes Where the choreography freezes, where close-ups come in Chapters 11 and 13
7 Check against the broadcast checklist Key, flash, red transitions Sections 17.4 and 17.5

Steps 1 and 2 come first because they let you pin down the song’s character fastest. When a new song comes out, watch the music video first and decide whether to put the lighting on the fast beat or the slow beat.1

The most common mistake is step 6. It seems that the more accents you add, the flashier it looks, but accents that jump out regardless of the camera cuts clutter the picture. Place accents where the choreography freezes and where the camera moves into a close-up. If you can get the camera script (the order of cuts), write it over the same bar numbers. When a lighting accent and a camera cut fall on the same beat, both come alive on screen.

Heard in the field

“Where does the choreography stop in this song?” The points you wrote in the song structure table (Chapters 3–5) and where the choreography stops are sometimes not the same. If you get the bars where the choreography stops from the choreography team and mark them separately in the song structure table, step 6 goes faster.

17.4 Four Camera Standards

For the stage to look right on screen, first look after four things: the key, the exposure of faces, the LED wall and flicker.

A scene the lighting director liked from the seats can be ruined on screen. So the looks on a music show are judged on the monitor, not from the seats. Bob Dickinson, lighting designer for the Grammy Awards, worked in the broadcast truck watching what the cameras saw, and according to the article he held the camera iris fixed and made the picture with lighting levels.3

1. Fix the key. Tie the key to one color temperature and do not change its level from song to song. Match the fixtures that carry the key to the same model and the same color temperature. Even at the same 5,600 K, a different model makes the face color look different on screen. Choose key fixtures with high color rendering (indexes such as CRI and TLCI).

2. Keep faces from washing out to white in dance sections. A singer’s face looking white in a dance section is a constant worry in music show lighting.1 When back and beams get strong and the camera lowers its exposure, the face goes dark, and if you correct that by raising the key, the face blows out to white. Before raising the key, first see whether you can bring the back and beams down one step. This is checking that “the key does not rise and fall with energy,” from Chapter 10, holds on screen.

3. The LED wall drags the color of the picture. When the LED wall fills a large part of the picture, the camera’s white balance and exposure are pulled toward that bright, broad surface. Match the LED wall’s brightness and main color with the video team scene by scene (the concept meeting questions in Chapter 7).

4. The camera sees flicker first. LED fixtures lower their brightness by switching the LED on and off quickly (PWM). If that frequency does not match the camera’s shutter, stripes (banding) or flicker that the eye cannot see appear on screen. Manufacturers let you change the LED frequency in the fixture menu, and advise finding the value where the stripes disappear while watching the camera or monitor.4 That value differs with each camera setting, so find it with the VE before the camera rehearsal and keep it uniform across the whole show.

Heard in the field

“Boss, the face is washing out.” When this comes from the camera side, before pulling the key down right away, first look at how bright the back, beams and LED wall are at that moment. Often the surroundings were bright and the picture got dark, so someone raised the key, or the iris was set for the face and only the face got too bright. The starting point is to bring the surroundings down one step and return the key to its original value.

17.5 The Broadcast Flash Standard

In broadcast, a large area of the picture must not flash more than three times a second.

ITU Recommendation BT.1702 and the guidance of Ofcom, the UK broadcast regulator, set this standard. The standard common to both documents is that an area larger than a quarter of the screen must not flash more than three times a second.5,6 The Ofcom guidance you saw in Chapter 10 adds two things. A change to a highly saturated red, or from red to another color, can be dangerous regardless of brightness, and flashing that continues for more than 5 seconds can also be dangerous.6 Check each domestic broadcaster’s own standard with that broadcaster.[Author to confirm]

It is easier to use if you convert this standard into the song’s beats. Three times a second is the speed of one flash per beat in a 180 BPM song (Calculation). So in most songs flashes on the beat are within the standard, and eighth-note flashes are outside it over a large area.

Table 17-4 Flash speed at each tempo Calculation
Song tempo Every beat Every eighth note Every sixteenth note
90 BPM 1.5 Hz 3.0 Hz 6.0 Hz
112 BPM(LOVE ATTACK) 1.9 Hz 3.7 Hz 7.5 Hz
128 BPM 2.1 Hz 4.3 Hz 8.5 Hz
150 BPM 2.5 Hz 5.0 Hz 10.0 Hz

An eighth note at 90 BPM (3.0 Hz) is exactly three times a second, so it sits on the boundary. Do not count a boundary value as within the standard; change it to one step slower. The starting values for actual use are these.

  • Strobes and blindersOver a large area, only up to beat units. If fast flashing is truly needed, use it only over a small area in a wide shot, only at the back of the stage.
  • RedAvoid bumps that go back and forth between red and black, even at a low speed. If you need a red accent, switch it to between red and white, or lower the saturation.
  • LengthCut a flash over a large area so that it does not exceed 5 seconds (about 2.3 bars at 112 BPM, Calculation).
A short experiment
  1. Start a stopwatch, play a stretch of a stage video you like where the strobe fires, and count how many times it flashes in one second.
  2. If it flashes more than three times and over more than a quarter of the screen, by broadcast standards that is a place to fix.

17.6 Using the Same Console Page for Every Song

On a music show you make one cue list per song, and keep the button layout of the console page the same for every song. Even when the performing act changes, if the buttons stay in the same places there are fewer mistakes.

The cue list format you built in Chapter 16 pays off here.

  • First and ending cues in the same placeThe first button is that song’s first look, and the last is the ending and the transition cue handing over to the next act (Chapter 14).
  • The safety look in the same placeAlways put the safety look (the base look) to call when you miss a cue, and the freeze, on the same executors (Chapter 21).
  • The key stays off the pagePut the key on a fixed fader, regardless of the song’s page (Chapter 13).
  • MR songs and live bandsA song done with MR (a recorded backing track) can be reproduced with timecode, and when a live band plays you press GO by hand. The console-side methods are covered in Part 5.

17.7 [Practice] LOVE ATTACK as a Music Show Stage

Check the lighting you built for LOVE ATTACK in Parts 2 and 3 against this chapter’s broadcast standards.

LOVE ATTACK used no strobes or blinders from the start, so little is caught by the flash standard. Even so, look separately at the red, the key and the LED wall.

Follow along

  1. From the cue sheet (Chapter 16), pull out every cue that flashes or changes by snap.
  2. For each cue, write the beat unit of the flash, the area it takes on screen, how long it lasts, and whether red is involved.
  3. Calculate Hz with Table 17-4 and mark the rows that are outside the standard.
  4. Check that no cue includes the key (that the key is outside the song cues).
  5. Write down the section where the LED wall gets brightest, matched to the video team’s cues, and mark the key in that section as a place to check on camera.
How to check
  1. For each marked row, write which of these fixes you will use: “slow it to beat units,” “reduce the area,” “change red↔white” or “cut within 5 seconds.”
  2. If none of the fixes fits, take that accent out of the broadcast and replace it with another accent means (Table 13-3 in Chapter 13).

This song’s risk lies not in flashes but in red and the LED wall. The red heart on bar 47 is held for two bars without flickering, so it is within the standard, but it is a place where a question may come up in the broadcaster’s check. If you write in the concept sheet (Chapter 7) why this red is needed, that is your answer.

Try it with your own song
  1. Pull out every flashing cue from the cue sheet of the song you are working on now and build the same table.
  2. If a row falls outside the standard, compare in rehearsal a version with that accent slowed to beat units and a version swapped for another accent means.

Key points

  • The audience of a music show is the camera. Judge looks on the monitor, not from the seats.
  • Time to prepare each song is tight, and chances to fix are one or two rehearsals. Compress the process of Parts 1–3 into seven steps and prepare in order.
  • You cannot pick fixture types for each song, so divide roles so that the day’s fixtures can handle many genres, and run the key separately.
  • Four camera standards: a fixed key, faces washing out to white, the LED wall, flicker.
  • Keep a large area from flashing more than three times a second, and avoid the boundary of exactly three as well. For most songs, beat units are in and eighth notes are out. Avoid red transitions and flicker lasting more than 5 seconds as well.
  • Use a console page with the same layout for every song, and always put the safety look and the key in the same places.
On the job tomorrow
  1. At the next rehearsal, turn everything off except the fixtures carrying the key.
  2. If the face looks right on the monitor with the key alone, the face will not fall apart whatever you put on top of it.
