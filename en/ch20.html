Chapter 20

Light Shows and Openings

When the light is the star

In this chapter
  • On a stage with no people, make a group of fixtures the main, and build the show in an order of solos.
  • Learn the light show starting value: “moving lights in white, color effects held still.”
  • Read the structure of the author’s light show (5 minutes 12 seconds) and divide its parts with dark cues.
Opening scene

Just before the show begins, the house lights go out and there is nobody on stage. Music sounds, and a show of several minutes unfolds with light alone. Beams shoot into the sky and gather into a single point, the pixel wall behind the stage ripples like a wave, and for a moment everything goes out and then bursts again. For those few minutes the star of the stage is not a person but the equipment.

In Parts 1–3 we said “keep the key on until the song ends” and “one main per scene.” On a stage with no people the key is not needed, and the only thing that can be the main is equipment. This chapter deals with that stage. Concert openings, intermissions between shows and event light shows all belong here.

20.1 The Main on a Stage with No People

On a stage with no people, one group of fixtures becomes the main (the star) of the scene.

In Chapter 11, inside a main role (beams, for example), fixture groups (floor beam movers, front truss spot movers) took solos four bars at a time. In a light show the fixture group that took the solo becomes the main of the scene as it is (the tower’s beam movers, the ceiling’s spot movers, the floor movers, the pixel wall).

In a light show the principle “one main per scene” becomes stricter. In haze (a thin smoke sprayed so that beams show), when the beams of several groups overlap, none of them can be seen. So for each section you light one main group almost alone, and keep the rest off or very low.1 If in a song you changed the solo every four bars inside the main role (Chapter 11), in a light show the starting value is to change the fixture group that holds the main for each part. It is the solo rotation of Chapter 11 widened to the whole show.

20.2 Setting the Solo Order for Each Group

A light show is built by setting an order so that the fixture groups take solos in turn. There are three starting values for setting the order.1

  • The same group comes back several times. If there are five or six kinds of fixtures, in a five-minute show the same group becomes the main several times. If you change the sub group lit with it each time it returns, the same equipment becomes a different scene.
  • Save the lead group. Use the lead group that is the face of the show (in this show, the 48 beam movers of the tower) for the big moments of the show, and give the first section to other groups (Starting value). It is the same as not showing every show special in the first song in Chapter 18.
  • Open everything at the end. In the finale, for the first time, all groups light together. It is the same as using the “moment when every role is on at once” only once in a song in Chapter 10.

20.3 Moving Lights in White, Color Effects Held Still

The starting value of a light show is to show moving and color effects only one at a time. When the beams move widely, take out the color and show the path in white, and when you show a two-color chase or a dimmer effect, stop the movement.1

If the color changes in haze while the beams are also moving, the audience can read neither the path nor the color.

This starting value follows the same order as the three acts of the solo in Chapter 9 (position / color and dimmer / beam).

  1. Position: white, a pan/tilt effect with a large amplitude.
  2. Color and dimmer: set up with a position preset, then a two-color snap or a dimmer effect.
  3. Beam: set aimed at the front of the audience, then gobo and prism rotation with the zoom opened wide.

This rule comes from arena light shows where the equipment is the main. It can differ on a pop stage where a person sings. A movement that draws a wave over pastel colors, like the chorus of LOVE ATTACK, differs from this rule, but it is natural on a stage centered on the key and the song. As we saw in Chapter 15, this is an adjustment value that you change according to the genre and the venue.

After the fix In the first 16 seconds of Part 7, a single pan/tilt effect was applied once on a 4-beat cycle, and after that the position was never called again. For 8 seconds a large vertical wave, and for the next 8 seconds a double spiral circle, were drawn to the end.1

20.4 Dividing the Parts with Dark Cues

A light show has no people and no song, so the lighting itself must tell the audience where the part changes. The surest way is a dark cue that empties the stage for a moment.

On a stage with people we kept the key (Chapter 10), but in a light show with no people you can also use a full blackout.

This show has four blackouts (75.2 s, 115.0 s, 168.3 s, 219.5 s).1 Three close a part, and one (219.5 s) sits in Part 6 right before the biggest hit. Two of the four are set long, right before a drop (the moment the music bursts all at once) or the biggest hit.

  • Blackout at 115.0 s, then 3.2 s (about 1.8 bars) of darkness until the drop at 118.2 s.
  • Blackout at 219.5 s, then 2.8 s (about 1.6 bars) of darkness until the biggest hit at 222.3 s.

In this song, where one bar is 1.76 seconds (136 BPM, Calculation), darkness of more than 1.5 bars is fairly long. In Chapter 12 the dark cue right before a drop was set at “the last 1 beat to 1 bar.” In a light show you may stretch it longer, enough to make the audience hold their breath. There is no song and no person, so even when the darkness is long the stage does not feel as if it has disappeared.

20.5 Breather Parts Inside a Show

A light show needs breather parts too, so that the drops feel big. In this show, Part 3 and Part 5 are those places.

Both are low-energy sections that start with the ceiling’s spot movers. They are slow, wide scenes, such as a white sweep passing slowly at 2-second intervals and cathedral beams in blue and white. Part 4 and Part 6, which come after them, are the drops.

The same structure as the breather song that made the next block big in the set list of Chapter 18 is inside a five-minute show. If you run at full from start to finish because the light show is short, the audience gets used to that brightness before the five minutes are up.

20.6 [Exercise] A Solo Order Table for Your Own Light Show

Build an order table that decides when each fixture group takes a solo and with which sub group. When it is done you can see where the same group comes up too often and where the lead group is used too early.

Follow along

  1. Write the groups of the rig (tower, ceiling, floor, pixel wall and so on). Mark the lead group that will be the face of the show.
  2. Divide the sections with the song structure table (Chapters 3–5) and give each section an energy level. Decide on at least two breather sections.
  3. For each section choose one main group and one sub group (if any). When the same group returns, change the sub group.
  4. For each main group decide which to show: position → color and dimmer → beam.
  5. Decide the places and lengths of the blackouts. Try stretching the blackout before a drop to longer than one bar.
How to check
  1. If the place where the lead group first becomes the main in the order table is within the first third of the show, see whether you can move it later.
  2. Mark any place where all the groups light at once before the finale.

Key points

  • On a stage with no people, the fixture group that took the solo in Chapter 11 becomes the main of the scene as it is. Changing the main group for each part is the starting value.
  • The same group returns several times but with a different sub group, the lead group is saved, and everything lights for the first time in the finale.
  • The starting value of a light show is “moving lights in white, color effects held still.” On a pop stage it is an adjustment value.
  • A light show with no people and no song divides its parts with blackouts. A blackout before a drop can be stretched to longer than one bar.
  • Put breather parts in a five-minute show too.
On the job tomorrow
  1. In your next opening or light show, count the number of groups lit in each section.
  2. If three or more are always on, make a version for just one section with only one main group left on and the rest off, and compare.
