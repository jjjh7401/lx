Chapter 19

Award Shows and Large TV Shows

A stage that changes with every segment

In this chapter
  • Split a show that alternates performance, awards and hosting into a different lighting mode for each part.
  • On a stage with short rehearsals and different demands from each act, divide what you share from what each act has of its own: common equipment and per-act cues.
Opening scene

Watch a year-end awards show on television and the air of the screen changes every few minutes. When one act’s performance ends, the intense color drains away and a presenter walks out onto a bright, white stage. When the winner is called, the camera turns to faces in the audience, and when the speech ends it goes dark again and the first note of the next act rings out.

The same stage, the same rig, and every few minutes it becomes a completely different show. An awards show is a stage where the space between one part and the next matters more than the staging of any one song. You keep the camera standards of Chapter 17 as they are, but you must change modes far more often than in the set list of Chapter 18.

19.1 A Different Lighting Mode for Each Part

At an awards show three parts alternate — performance, awards and hosting — and each part uses the lights in a different way (a mode). If you divide the modes in advance, then even with short rehearsals you will not be confused about what to change when the part changes.

Table 19-1 The parts of an awards show and their lighting modes Starting value [Author to confirm]
Part Main Key Color Moving
Performance The artist’s song Performance key (fixed) That song’s palette Depends on the song
Awards Presenters and winners Award key (fixed, white) One main color of the show, low saturation Almost still
Hosting (MC) The host MC key (fixed) The show’s main color Still
Transition The next part Hold Dark cue or white reset (Chapter 14) Next position by MIB

The white of the awards part is the reference point of the show. However strong a color passes through the performance parts, when you come back to the awards part the same white and the same key are waiting. By that white viewers know “this is the awards part now.”

19.2 Lighting That Fits the Camera

At an awards show the camera comes before the lighting. Decide the standards for brightness and color with the camera side before the show, and during the show the lighting matches those standards.

An awards show is even more thoroughly a show for the camera than the music show of Chapter 17. Bob Dickinson, the lighting designer of the Grammy Awards seen in Chapter 17, kept the camera iris fixed and made the picture with lighting levels. As long as the video operator did not change the camera, he was said to be free to handle brightness with the lighting.1

If the camera changes its iris for every scene, the contrast of the lighting disappears. Conversely, if you hold the camera fixed, what the lighting makes dark is dark on screen and what it makes bright is bright on screen.

  • Reference values for the keyDecide the color temperature and brightness of the award key, MC key and performance key together with the VE (video engineer) before the show, and record them measured with a light meter or a waveform monitor (Chapter 10).
  • The seats matter lessIf a scene looks too bright or too dark from the seats but is right on screen, follow the screen. Even so, avoid any beam that shines straight into the eyes of the audience in the seats, whatever the screen shows.

19.3 Short Rehearsals and Different Demands from Each Act

At an awards show where many acts appear and each has a short rehearsal, divide the equipment and cues into what the whole show shares and what each act has to itself. Let us call the first the common layer and the second the team layer.

  • Common layerThe key, the awards and hosting modes, the transition cues, and the opening and ending that the whole show shares. The show’s lighting director finishes them before the show.
  • Team layerEach act’s song cue list. Sometimes the act’s own lighting director or director comes separately, and sometimes the show’s lighting director writes it. The team layer does not touch the key and transitions of the common layer (Chapter 13, “one sequence owns one attribute of one fixture”).

If a director is bringing in lighting built separately for an act, hand over three things before rehearsal: the fixture role table (Chapter 6), the values of the common key, and the flash standard (Section 17.5). Without these three, half the rehearsal time goes to “what does this fixture do?”

19.4 Rig That Lights the Whole Venue

At an awards show it is not only the stage but the whole venue that appears on screen. Awards shows are usually held in large venues, and the cameras often show wide shots that take in the seats as well.

So part of the rig is used to light not the stage but the whole venue. Trusses over the seats and fixtures placed around the edge of the venue tie the venue together into one picture in wide shots.

In the performance parts these fixtures act as beams or audience lights, and in the awards parts they wrap the venue in the show’s main color. If you save them for moments with wide shots — the opening, a highlight stage, the announcement of the grand prize — they become show specials (Chapter 18).

19.5 Winners’ Paths and Audience Reactions

In the awards parts the lighting follows people. When the winner is called they stand up in the seats and walk to the stage, and the camera follows that path and the reactions of the audience.

  • The moment of the callPrepare audience lights or follow spots in advance at the nominees’ seats. If you cannot know which seat until the name is called, place a weak key evenly at every nominee’s seat.
  • The pathMake sure the way from the seats to the stage is not dark. A follow spot picks up the winner and, once they are on stage, hands them over to the award key.
  • Audience reaction shotsSet the audience lights so that faces in the seats can be seen on screen. If it is too bright the audience looks like the stage, and if it is too dark the reaction shots become useless. Decide the level at the camera rehearsal.

19.6 [Exercise] The Part Timeline of One Awards Show

Build the part timeline of one made-up awards show (six performing acts, five awards, hosting). Decide the mode for each part, and write what changes at each transition.

Follow along

  1. Take the run sheet of the show and write the parts in order (performance, awards, hosting, video).
  2. Decide the mode of Table 19-1 for each part. For performance parts, write the act and the song.
  3. Between each pair of parts, choose a transition tool (Chapter 14) and write what you will move by MIB.
  4. Write the color temperature and level of the award key, MC key and performance key on one line.
  5. Decide where to reveal the show specials (the scene that lights the whole venue, the grand prize announcement).
How to check
  1. Read the timeline from start to finish and see whether every return to an awards part comes back to the same key and the same white.
  2. Where an act’s cue touches the award key, take the key out of that cue and leave it in the common layer.

If LOVE ATTACK were one performance part of an awards show, the transitions before and after the song would matter as much as the staging inside it. Before the song, drop from the white of the awards part to a dark cue and then open the Pearl Heart of the intro with a snap (Chapter 14). After the song, finish the end-section fade (Chapter 12) and hand over to the MC key while the applause continues. The pastel of pearl pink shows up more clearly when the acts before and after are in primary colors.[Author to confirm]

Try it with your own song
  1. Make the same timeline with the run sheet of the show you are working on now.
  2. Mark the places where a transition is shorter than 30 seconds, and check that there is not too much to move by MIB in those transitions.

Key points

  • An awards show is a show where performance, awards and hosting alternate. First divide a lighting mode for each part.
  • The white and the fixed key of the awards part are the show’s reference point. By that white viewers know it is the awards part.
  • Decide the camera and lighting standards together before the show, and during the show the lighting matches them.
  • Divide equipment and cues into a common layer and a team layer, and the team layer does not touch the common key and transitions.
  • The lighting also looks after the winner’s path and the audience reaction shots.
On the job tomorrow
  1. In the run sheet of your next show, pick out every awards (or hosting) part and write the key value of that part on one line.
  2. At rehearsal, check that it comes back to that value whenever it returns during the show.
