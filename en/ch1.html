Reading the Song

Chapter 1

Listening Three Times

Listening notes, and the light that fits each sound

In this chapter
  • Listen to a new song in three separate passes, noting something different each time.
  • Build the habit of hearing an instrument and immediately thinking of the light that goes with it.
  • Write your listening notes in bars, not seconds.
Opening scene

When you listen to a song you love, there's a moment when you hold your breath because you can tell it's about to explode. The sound builds little by little just before the chorus, then everything pours out at once. Even on a song you've never heard, you can feel that moment coming.

That is exactly when the stage lighting has to explode along with the music. While the audience is waiting for it, the lighting director already has to know where it is. That preparation starts with listening, not at the console.

Terms to know first

This chapter uses a few terms that Chapters 2 and 3 cover in detail. Here are their short meanings.

Song · music · vocals
In this book a song means any single piece of music, with or without singing. An EDM track is a song too. Music means the whole flow of sound, and vocals means only the parts with a human voice and lyrics.
BPM
How many beats come in one minute. At 112 BPM there are 112 beats per minute (Chapter 2).
Bar · downbeat
A bar is a group of beats (usually four in popular music), and the first beat of a bar is the downbeat (Chapter 2).
Cue
One lighting state, plus how to get there, stored together on the console. A list of cues in order is a cue sheet.
Hit
A single moment where the music strikes hard, or the short lighting change you put on that moment.
Verse · pre-chorus · chorus
The part that tells the story, the part that builds anticipation before the chorus, and the centerpiece of the song (Chapter 3).
Build-up · drop
In EDM, the part that builds anticipation and the part that hits hardest (Chapter 3).

1.1 Turning music into something you can see

Lighting's job is to make the music you hear visible. James L. Moody, author of the classic Concert Lighting, called concert lighting a "visual translation" of the music. He also wrote that lighting should make the song stand out, never bury it.2

To translate, you first have to read the original. For a lighting director, the original is the song. You need to get three things out of the song before you can write cues.

  1. When it changes: section boundaries, hits, tempo changes
  2. What changes: which instruments come in and drop out
  3. How it feels: the emotion and color each section brings to mind

Try to catch all three in one listen and you won't catch any of them properly. So you split the listening.

1.2 Three separate listens

Don't try to get everything from a new song at once. Look for something different each time you listen.

Moody said he listens to a new song the way he reads a script. You read a script for fun the first time, look for the stage directions that need light the second time, and find the cues hidden in the dialogue the third time. You listen to a song three times the same way.2

Listen 1 — Enjoy it. Listen from start to finish without writing anything down. Don't stop partway. You just want the flow of the song to sink in. That flow is the impression the audience gets when they hear the song for the first time.

Listen 2 — Find cue points. This time, take notes as you listen. Mark where sections change, where the big hits land and where the tempo shifts. You can stop and rewind. What you get from this pass becomes the skeleton of the cue sheet.

Listen 3 — Feel its colors. Listen with your eyes closed and write down the colors each section brings to mind. Moody wrote that a bright, happy song suggests yellow and orange, a sad song blue and lavender, a hot song red, and a relaxed song green and teal.2 These pairings are a starting point, not the right answer. Color planning is covered in Chapter 8.

Table 1-1 Listening time per pass, for a 3-minute song Starting value
Pass What you listen for What you write Time
1 The overall flow of the song Nothing 3 min
2 Boundaries, hits, tempo changes Bar numbers and changes 10–15 min (including rewinds)
3 Emotion and color One or two colors per section 3–5 min

All three passes take about 20 minutes. Those 20 minutes cut down the time you spend fixing things in rehearsal.

Quick experiment
  1. Play any song on your phone and listen to the end without writing anything, like Listen 1.
  2. When it's over, write down roughly where the biggest moment was.
  3. In Listen 2, find that spot and check how close you were.

1.3 Music videos and dance practice videos

The quickest way to see which beat the performers move to is a music video or a dance practice video. It's hard to tell from the audio alone.

Even within one song, there are sections where the choreography moves in small steps on a fast beat and sections where it moves in big strokes on a slow beat. If the lighting follows a different beat from the choreography, the stage looks split in two. This is the same idea as the "felt tempo" covered in Chapter 2.

There are four things to look for in a music video.

  • The beat the choreography followsIs it the fast beat or the slow one, and does it change from section to section?
  • Where the signature move happensWrite down which bar the most memorable move of the song lands on. That's also a place for one big lighting change.
  • The colors on screenThe colors of the music video are a good reference when choosing colors for the song. You don't have to copy them, though (Chapter 7, Concept).
  • Faces in close-upOn a stage that goes out through cameras to a screen, it's a failure if the faces are dark, however impressive the background. Material on K-pop TV music show lighting also lists this as a common mistake.3

1.4 Matching sound and light

Each sound, such as the kick, the bass or the vocal, has a kind of light that suits it.

During Listen 2 you'll get the feeling that "something changes here." To turn that feeling into a cue, you need to know what changed. The light you use depends on whether the kick came in, the bass dropped out, or the vocal was left on its own.

Figure 1-2 collects these sound-and-light pairings. They are starting values to try first, not right answers.

The pairings are easier to remember as groups.

  • Rhythm (kick, snare, hi-hat) pairs with fast, short light. Movements that finish within one beat, such as intensity pulses, short flashes and finely flickering pixels. This is where the beat math from Chapter 2 gets used the most.
  • Weight and harmony (bass, pads, chords) pair with slow, wide light. The intensity and color of the washes that cover the stage. They change slowly, section by section.
  • Melody and people (lead melody, vocals) pair with lines and faces. Moving light beams follow the line of the melody, while the front light and follow spots look after the singer's face.
  • The sounds of change (risers, fills, crashes, drops) pair with light you use only a few times per song. Blinders and full-stage hits only land if you save them (Chapter 10).

Hearing a sound doesn't mean you turn on its pairing, though. In a chorus where the kick, snare, hi-hat and bass all play together, moving all four kinds of light makes the stage louder than the music. The pairings are a list to choose from, and Chapters 11 and 13 decide which ones to use.

Quick experiment
  1. Play the same song and this time follow only the bass.
  2. Ignore everything else and mark with your finger only when the bass comes in and drops out.
  3. When the bass drops out, the song often takes a short breath.
  4. That's a good moment to bring the floor intensity down too.

1.5 Notes in bars, not seconds

In your listening notes, write which bar something happens in, not the minute and second.

Still, everyone starts by writing times, because the number on the player screen is a time. The left side of Figure 1-3 is a note written that way.

Both notes describe the same song and the same part. But a few things jump out of the note on the right.

  • You can see how long each section is. The verse is 7 bars, and the pre-chorus (build-up) is 4 bars. With the note on the left, you'd have to subtract 0:14 from 0:29 and divide by the length of one bar (about 2.14 seconds).
  • You can see the repeats. In verse 1, the pre-chorus, chorus and kick-out land on bars 14, 18 and 33. In verse 2 they land on bars 42, 46 and 61, exactly 28 bars later. You can copy the verse 2 cues from verse 1.
  • It carries straight into the cue sheet and the console. As Chapter 2 shows, once you have the bar number you can always calculate the time. If you write only seconds, you have to redo the notes for a remix or a live version where the BPM changes.

To write in bars, you have to be able to count bars. Starting from the first downbeat, count "one, two, three, four" and fold down a finger at the end of each bar. Chapter 2 covers how to count accurately and how to find the first beat.

How to check
  1. Once your listening notes are done, use the length of verse 1 to predict verse 2.
  2. In LOVE ATTACK, verse 1 runs from bar 7, where Verse 1 starts, to bar 33, just before the second verse starts: 27 bars.
  3. Add 27 to Chorus 1 (bar 18), and the second chorus should start at bar 45.
  4. But when you listen, it starts at bar 46.
  5. It's off by one bar, because Verse 2 is one bar longer than Verse 1.
  6. Places where the math and what you hear don't line up are the special points of a song (Chapter 3).

BPM · first beat Measured with the method in Chapter 2 112 BPM, first downbeat at 1.50 s

Music video Beat the choreography follows, bar of the signature move

The last field matters. Once you write down which of the things you heard on your own are certain and which are guesses, you know what to check first in rehearsal. The song map in Chapter 5 expands on this template.

One review of LOVE ATTACK describes the song as opening on distorted guitar, with the drum machine coming in before the melody. It says the drums take center stage in the chorus, and that the song includes progressive house elements and a beat drop.4,5 Progressive house is a style of electronic dance music that builds the sound up gradually, and a beat drop is the passage where the vocals drop out and the beat takes the lead (Chapter 3). Compare this description with the Listen 2 changes in your notes to double-check what you heard.

Key points

  • Lighting is a translation that makes music visible. Read the original, the song, first.
  • Listen to a new song three times: to enjoy it, to find cue points and to feel its colors.
  • Use the music video to check the beat the choreography follows and where the signature move lands.
  • Each sound has a light that pairs with it. Rhythm pairs with short, fast light; weight and harmony with wide, slow light; melody and people with lines and faces; and the sounds of change with light you save.
  • Write listening notes in bar numbers. The repeats become visible, and the notes carry straight into the cue sheet.
On the job tomorrow
  1. The next time you get a song, listen to it three times before you turn on the console.
  2. Write the first line of your Listen 2 notes as "bar so-and-so," not as a time.
