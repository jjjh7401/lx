Chapter 32

Safety and Regulations

Flashing, rigging and special effects

In this chapter
  • Calculate the flashing standards for broadcast and for live shows from the song’s BPM and Measure, and filter out cues that exceed them at the cue sheet stage.
  • Sort out what a lighting director should agree in advance about beams and lasers aimed at the audience, haze and special effects.
  • Put the safety work that touches lighting design on the preparation schedule: fixing and loading of hung gear, and the filing of the performance notice.
Opening scene

When a camera flash goes off right in front of you, a round spot floats before your eyes for a while. It mostly fades after a few seconds, but for those few seconds you cannot see well. Before light is a nice picture, it is energy that reaches people’s eyes and bodies.

Stage light is the same. Parts 1 to 5 were about how to use light beautifully. This chapter is about the standards that keep that light from harming the audience, the performers and the broadcast viewers. We go in order: how fast light flashes, beams aimed at the audience’s eyes, haze and special effects, and gear hung overhead.

These standards are not set by the lighting director alone. The broadcaster, the venue, the stage manager, the special-effects crew and the rigging crew who hang the gear set them together. What the lighting director does is know in advance which standards apply to the design, filter cues out at the cue sheet stage, and talk early with the person in charge.

32.1 Flashing Standards for Broadcast

A stage that goes out on broadcast should not have an area larger than a quarter of the screen flash more than three times a second. The ITU recommendation and the guidance of Ofcom, the UK broadcast regulator, which we saw in Chapter 17, set this standard.1 2

There is a case where this standard was actually applied. In October 2010, on the talent show “The X Factor” on ITV1 in the UK, a pre-recorded performance by a singer was broadcast with laser lighting effects that changed and flashed rapidly. When Ofcom analyzed it technically, nearly 4 seconds of a stretch of just over 5 and a half seconds went beyond the photosensitive epilepsy standard. The broadcaster had given a warning before the broadcast, but Ofcom held that a warning is no substitute for an inadequate pre-check, and found a breach of the rules.3

A lighting director can take three lessons from this case.

  • A lighting effect is also flashing video on screen. The standard is judged not by how the picture was made but by how it came out on screen. Laser, strobe and LED wall are all the same.
  • Check a recorded stage before broadcast too. It was a pre-recorded stage, not a live one, and it was still found in breach.
  • A warning alone is not enough. Even if the flashing is essential to the staging, first reduce it to within the standard.

If you take on a broadcast stage, get that broadcaster’s own flashing standard first. If you receive none, it is safer to use the ITU and Ofcom standards.

32.2 Strobes at a Venue

At a live venue that is not broadcast, the reference standard is strobes (short, strong flashes of light) at no more than four times a second. This is the value the UK Health and Safety Executive (HSE) recommended for club strobes, and the UK epilepsy charity Epilepsy Society presents it.

The same charity also says that light flashing 3 to 30 times a second is the range most likely to provoke a seizure.4 Four times a second also falls inside this range, so four is not a “safe value” but an upper limit not to be exceeded.

How fast light flashes can be calculated in advance at the cue sheet stage with the formula of Chapter 25. Flashes per second = BPM ÷ 60 ÷ Measure (Calculation).

Two things show in the table. First, flashing in sixteenth notes goes over the stage standard in every song faster than 60 BPM. A staging that hammers the strobe in sixteenths on the drop cannot be used within this standard. Second, for half-beat units, 120 BPM is the border. In songs faster than that, even a half-beat strobe goes over the stage standard. The half-beat chase used for the 120–135 BPM drop in Table 12-2 of Chapter 12 should also be checked again against this standard when it is used as a strobe or a white flash.

This calculation counts how many times one fixture flashes in a second (Section 25.3). With a phased chase (Phase split), each fixture on its own may be within the standard, but from the seats the light bursts across the whole stage more often than that. The broadcast standard too is judged not per fixture but by adding up all the areas flashing at the same time on screen,7 so on a broadcast stage you look at the flashing of the whole camera picture. For a live show too, strobes and blinders that cover the audience widely are judged by the count of the whole stage.[Author to confirm]

A show that uses strobes tells the audience in advance. NODA, the UK association of amateur theatre groups, recommends that when strobes are used you always post a notice at the venue entrance and, where possible, print it in the programme as well, and says not to use them for more than 30 seconds at a time.8 If you tell people through the booking information or an announcement before the show, light-sensitive audience members can prepare themselves.

32.3 Beams and Lasers Aimed at the Audience

Use light aimed at the audience’s eyes briefly, and only in places decided beforehand. Blinders (lights that flash strongly toward the audience all at once) and beams aimed at the audience pull the audience into the show, but if they stay on for long the eyes are dazzled and the audience cannot see the stage.

Do not aim a very bright beam directly at the audience’s eye level. Quite a few of today’s LED moving lights and LED walls, depending on distance and output, can fall in RG2 to RG3 of the photobiological risk groups (the grades that sort the risk light poses to the eyes and skin, IEC 62471), and repeating a narrow beam on the audience or holding it there for long is counted as a hazardous situation.9 So for cues that use blinders and the audience positions of beam movers (positions that light the audience area), you fix the length in the cue sheet. Use them in a short moment, like one hit or the first bar of a chorus, and avoid a position that stays on one seat for long. The front rows are close to the stage and receive the same beam much brighter, so set audience positions to pass over the audience’s heads.[Author to confirm]

Lasers are stricter. In a joint statement in 2017, the radiation safety authorities of several Nordic countries advised avoiding shooting high-power lasers (Class 3B and 4) directly at the audience or scanning them toward the audience. They said the light the audience receives must in no case exceed the maximum permissible exposure (MPE). If an effect that reaches the audience is used, they also said to measure beforehand, at the spot that receives the strongest light, for each show, and to keep those safety measures in writing.5

Rules on how much laser light may reach an audience differ by country and by venue, and I did not find one common public standard for laser performances. So the lighting director talks early with the laser company rather than handling the laser directly. In the cue sheet, write down together whether any cue sends the laser toward the audience, and what measurement and safety devices the company prepares for that cue.

Words to Know
Maximum permissible exposure
(MPE): The largest amount of laser light that the eye or skin can receive without harm. It varies with the laser’s strength, its color (wavelength) and the time of exposure, so the company measures it and judges.

32.4 Haze and Special Effects

Staging that uses haze or flames is used within the conditions set by the venue’s fire officer and the special-effects crew. The lighting director carries those conditions into the cue sheet and agrees who gives the call, and when.

Haze makes beams visible in the air, but it can set off smoke detectors. As one example, the Kelvin Hall studio of BBC Studioworks has a set procedure for using haze. First a permit is submitted, then a designated person isolates the detector for a time and everyone is told over the intercom and the studio announcement. When the smoke has cleared, the detector is restored and signed off.6

Smoke detectors are fire-protection equipment, and disabling or blocking them is governed by local fire rules. So at a venue where haze will be used, the lighting side does not handle the detectors. Before rehearsal, agree the following with the venue’s fire safety manager.

  • The kind of detectors at the venue and the conditions under which haze can be used
  • The amount of haze and the time it is used (rehearsal and the show)
  • Ventilation between performances and after the end
  • Who gives notice, and how, when a fire is suspected

Flames and special effects are run by the company in charge, but those cues move together with the lighting cues. Outdoors, wind changes what is safe, so agree with the company beforehand the wind speed at which it holds back or stops an effect, and who watches the wind in real time. For flame effects and flammable confetti, leave time intervals or use flame-retardant ones, and control the zone where debris will fall. The lighting director marks the special-effects cues separately in the cue sheet, and decides in advance who gives the call for those cues (Chapter 31), and how to change the lighting cues if an effect is dropped because of wind.

32.5 Hung Gear and Power

Gear hung overhead is fixed twice over, and the gear weight and power data are handed to the person in charge in advance. Rigging and load calculations are the job of the rigging crew and the stage manager, but the basis of those calculations is the equipment list the lighting director decided (Chapter 22).

A hanging tool weakens a great deal once it is damaged. A wire rope with even one kink, for example, no longer holds the load it was rated for, so the rigging crew takes damaged tools out of use. That is why every piece of hung gear carries a safety bond as well as its clamp.

What the lighting director hands over is the equipment list calculated in Chapter 22. Hand over, in a table, the weight of the hung gear, the load per hoist and the total power, and hand it over again if the gear changes. For the back truss of the Chapter 22 practice stage, the gear weight of 6 wash movers and 8 strobes is 228 kg, and splitting it over 4 hoists gives 57 kg per hoist (Calculation; the weight of the truss and cables is added separately). The power of all 94 fixtures of the practice stage is 42.10 kW. If a request comes during rehearsal to hang more gear, do not hang it on the spot; tell the rigging crew the weight first.

The performance notice also goes on the schedule. Many places require a notice or permit for large or outdoor shows, and some ask for a disaster response plan filed days ahead. Filing is the job of the producer or organizer, but the equipment and special-effects lists in the plan come from lighting and special effects. If that list is late, the filing is late. Ask the producer early which notices apply.

32.6 [Exercise] LOVE ATTACK Safety Check

Imagine putting LOVE ATTACK on both a broadcast stage and a live stage, and check the flashing, the beams aimed at the audience, the haze and the hung gear.

Follow along

  1. Load the phasers of Tables 25-3 and 25-4 in Chapter 25 and the accent means of Chapter 13 (the brightness hit and the white flash) into Web tool 25.
  2. At 112 BPM, see how many times a second each flashing cue flashes, and sort them by the broadcast standard (three times) and the live standard (four times). “Odd/even, fast” and “Sparkle” in Table 25-3 (Measure 0.5, 3.7 times for one fixture) go over the broadcast standard, and by one fixture they do not go over the live standard. But in odd/even the two groups light in turn, so the whole stage bursts about 7.5 times a second, and it is looked at again for the live show too (Calculation).
  3. Find in the cue sheet the cues where beams point toward the audience, and write their lengths. LOVE ATTACK uses no blinders or audience beams (Table 13-3 in Chapter 13), so only check whether the chorus fan or the beat section’s cross beams pass over the audience.
  4. Write the sections that use haze and the amount, and mark the items to ask the venue (the four of Section 32.4).
  5. From the practice rig in Web tool 18, the Rig Calculator and Patch Sheet, load the weight of the hung gear and the total power, and make the table to hand to the rigging crew.
How to check
  1. Compare the cue lists of the broadcast version and the live version.
  2. If you change the cue caught in the broadcast version (3.7 times) to Measure 1 (1.9 times), does it come within the standard? Write what will give the scene its force in place of the reduced flashing (size, color, area).
  3. If any cue aimed at the audience stays for more than a bar, shorten it.
Try it with your own stage
  1. From the cue sheet of the show you are in charge of now, pick only the flashing cues and the cues aimed at the audience and check them with Web tool 25.
  2. Print the items to send the venue (haze, special effects, hung gear, notice) on one sheet and send it before rehearsal.

Key points

  • For broadcast, an area larger than a quarter of the screen must not flash more than three times a second, and for live strobes the upper limit is four times a second, which the UK HSE recommended for clubs. Flashes per second = BPM ÷ 60 ÷ Measure.
  • Flashing in sixteenth notes goes over the live standard in songs faster than 60 BPM, and for half-beat units, 120 BPM is the border.
  • A lighting effect is also flashing video on screen. Check a recorded stage before broadcast too, and a warning before the broadcast does not replace a pre-check.
  • Use beams aimed at the audience briefly, and use lasers only under conditions the laser company has measured and agreed.
  • Agree haze with the venue’s fire safety manager, special effects with the company in charge, and hung gear with the rigging crew. The lighting director hands over the cues and the equipment list that these rest on early.
On the job tomorrow
  1. Before handing over the cue sheet, write the flashes per second beside each flashing cue.
  2. Look again at cues whose number is over 3 for broadcast, and cues over 4 for live shows.
Closing Part 6

In Part 6 we ran rehearsal and the show with the cues built on the console.

Before rehearsal we checked the values with previz (Chapter 28). In rehearsal we found where the simulation and the real stage part ways, wrote them in the notes and fixed them (Chapter 30). In the show we ran cues on the beat with standby and GO, and decided in advance the cue to go back to if a cue is missed (Chapter 31). Finally we checked the safety standards for the audience, the performers and the broadcast viewers (Chapter 32).

The work that began in Part 1 with listening to a song ends here. The order is to read the song (Part 1), design the light (Part 2), place it on beats and bars (Part 3), fit it to the site (Part 4), build it on the console (Part 5) and put it on stage (Part 6). The gear and people change from stage to stage, but this order does not change. With the next song, too, we start in the same order, by listening to the song first.
