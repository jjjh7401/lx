Chapter 12

Lighting Each Section

How the light changes from intro to chorus

In this chapter
  • Set starting values for each type of section: the roles that come on, color, movement, brightness and in time.
  • Use BPM to set the effect cycle, and energy to set how many roles and effects are on.
  • Program build-ups and drops, kick-out bars and the end of the song, in order.
Opening scene

Picture the end of a build-up in a venue. The drums grow denser, and the beams on stage rise one by one. Then, for a moment, every light goes out. The audience holds its breath for about a second. On the next downbeat the beams fan out all at once, and only then does the cheer burst.

That one second of darkness does not happen by chance. What you pile up in the build-up, what you take away just before the drop, and what you open all at once at the drop are already decided, because each section has its own job. If Chapter 11 set the musical unit each attribute follows, this chapter sets starting values for each section.

12.1 Starting Looks by Section

The look you grab first changes with the kind of section, verse or chorus.

A verse is where you let the song be heard, so the key is the center and movement is small. A chorus is the biggest place in the song, so nearly every role comes on. A bridge has to be a different scene from what came before, so you reduce the roles that are on and bring out a color you have not used yet. Gather these differences in one table and, even with a song you have just received, you can decide right away what to turn on in each section.

The brightness in the table is the starting value for that section’s main. The key keeps the same level for as long as the song continues, regardless of the table (Chapter 6). You do not have to use the table as it is. If you decide differently from a starting value, write a line beside the look sheet on why it differs. The practice in Section 12.7 is exactly that.

A short experiment
  1. Watch a concert video of a song you like with the sound off.
  2. Judging from the picture alone, guess whether it is a verse or a chorus right now.
  3. In a video that is easy to guess, the number of roles on clearly differs from section to section.
  4. In a video that is hard to guess, a similar number of roles are on from start to finish.

12.2 Effect Cycles by BPM

Whether the song is fast or slow (BPM) changes how many beats an effect should take to go around.

The same “2-beat cycle” is 1.5 s at 80 BPM and 0.94 s at 128 BPM (Calculation). A pulse that brightens and dims once per beat also looks like breathing in a slow song and like blinking in a fast one. So you divide BPM into a few zones (bands) and decide for each band how many beats an effect cycle takes.

Table 12-2 Effect cycle by tempo band — in beats Starting value 1
BPM band Dimmer effect Color effect Position effect One chase step
Under 80 (ballad) 4–8 beats, like breathing 8–16 beats 16–32 beats 1 bar
80–100 2–4 beats 4–8 beats 8–16 beats 1–2 beats
100–120 (medium dance pop) 1–4 beats 4–8 beats 4–8 beats 1 beat
120–135(EDM) 1–2 beats 2–4 beats 4–8 beats 1 beat, half a beat only at the drop
135–160 (rock, pop punk) 2 beats 4 beats 4–8 beats 1–2 beats
160 and above 2–4 beats (read as half time) 4–8 beats 8 beats 2 beats

There are two rules for reading the table. First, in every band the position effect is slower than the dimmer effect. It is the ladder of Chapter 11 stretched or shrunk to the tempo. Second, set the cycle so it divides the bar evenly, like 1, 2, 4 or 8 beats. A 3- or 5-beat cycle fits the beat but meets the first beat of a bar only once every few bars, so a shape that started on a downbeat sits in a different place on the next downbeat.

LOVE ATTACK is 112 BPM, so it falls in the 100–120 band. The dimmer effect starts at 1–4 beats (0.54–2.1 s), and the color and position effects at 4–8 beats (2.1–4.3 s) (Calculation). The “two-color snap, beats 2 and 4” of the chorus in the beat grid of Chapter 11 (Table 11-2) is not an effect that changes scene color but a rhythm layer of pixels set to the claps. So it follows the accent rule of Section 12.3 (beats 2 and 4, twice per bar) rather than this table.

Terms to know — flash speed
Hz (hertz)
How many times it blinks in one second. At 112 BPM, blinking once per beat is 1.87 Hz, and blinking every half beat is 3.73 Hz (Calculation).
Broadcast flash standards
Measured against the British Ofcom standard from Chapter 10 (no more than three flashes per second over an area larger than a quarter of the screen), a beat-level flash at 112 BPM is inside the limit and a half-beat flash is outside it. Check the standards of domestic broadcasters with each broadcaster (Chapters 17 and 32).

12.3 Accents on the Rhythm

You do not match the lights to every single drum sound. For each sound, you set a ceiling on which role answers it and how often.

An accent is like punctuation in writing. A text with an exclamation mark at every comma is hard to read.

Table 12-3 Accent by rhythm element Starting value 1
Rhythm element Light’s response Ceiling
Four-on-the-floor kick A dimmer pulse on the back or floor beams. In a verse only on beat 1 of the bar, in a chorus and a drop on all four beats Once per beat
Snare · claps (beats 2 and 4) A short bump on the sides, or a two-color snap on a narrow surface such as pixels Twice per bar
Hi-hat (8th · 16th) A left-right alternating chase on the sides (grouped by beat), or on a narrow surface in the biggest section For broadcast, three per second
Crash · shout A white flash, or the audience once About once every 4 bars
Riser (rising sound) A white hit every bar, with the last one long Once per bar
Drum fill Signals the next section (a chase speeding up, or a dip) Once
End of a sung line A short bump on the wash Once every 1–4 bars

“Verse kick on the first beat only, chorus kick on all four” is the heart of this table. Even for the same kick, in the verse you only point to the start of the bar, and only in the chorus do you answer on every beat. If you use all four beats from the start, there is nothing more to show in the chorus. It is the headroom of Chapter 10 applied to rhythm. Since LOVE ATTACK usually uses at most two rhythm layers, the kick layer is not turned on in Choruses 1 and 2, and the kick is used on every beat only in Chorus 3 (Chapter 13).

In LOVE ATTACK, the low bass (808) takes the back, the kick takes the floor beams, the hi-hat takes the sides, and the claps take the pixels. This method of assigning one fixture group to each instrument is covered in detail in Chapter 13.

12.4 Energy and Fixture Usage

The energy (1–5) you rated in Chapter 4 is expressed not by brightness but by the number of roles that are on and the number of effects running at once.

If you raise energy by brightness alone, you hit 100% already at 3, and there is nothing left to raise at 4 and 5. So instead of brightness, you divide energy by how many roles come on, how many effects run at once, and how wide the beams spread.

Table 12-4 Energy and fixture usage Starting value 1
Energy Roles on (including key) Effects running at once Beam width Example
1 Key + one 0 Narrow First line of an a cappella
2 Three to four 1–2 Narrow LOVE ATTACK intro, Verse 1, bridge
3 Four to five 2 Medium Pre-chorus, Verse 2
4 Six or more, nearly all 2 Wide Chorus, beat section
5 That round’s fixtures + one entry accent 2 (3 only at the peak of the song) + one momentary accent Widest Entry at bars 18 and 46, bar 67 beat 4 to bar 68

The table’s “effects running at once” is the same as the number of rhythm layers in Chapter 11. At energy 2–4 it does not exceed two, and instead energy is divided by the number of roles on and the beam width. How fast the effects run is set by the BPM band (Section 12.2). Three open only at the peak of the song.

Energy 5 is a moment, not a section. Just as Chapter 4 put energy 5 only three times in a song, the strongest means of entry (a brightness hit, a mover burst, a white flash) come out only at those three moments. Turning every role on at once is separate from that, and happens once inside the song’s highest section (Chapter 10). The rest of the choruses are carried by energy-4 fixtures.

12.5 Build-ups and Drops

In a build-up you add lights one by one, just before the drop you empty briefly, and at the drop you open everything at once.

Of the three steps, the one most often skipped is the middle one, emptying: the dark cue. If you go from a stage brightened by the build-up straight into the drop, the drop is only a little brighter.

Written as starting values, the order goes like this.

  1. Build-up (4–8 bars): Add a white hit or one role every bar. The movers begin to move 5–10 s before the drop. The color lowers in saturation and goes toward white.
  2. Dark cue (last 1 beat to 1 bar): The scene is brought down to the base only. The key is kept separately. This is the dark cue of Chapter 10. During this time the movers are moved in advance to the drop’s position (MIB, Section 14.3).
  3. Drop downbeat: Open wide with a snap. The beams take the widest position and the color is two contrasting colors.
  4. Hold (1 bar): Stop on one position preset to show the shape. As you saw in Chapter 9, movement looks big when it contrasts with stillness.
  5. Effects resume: Run the effects from the second bar.

LOVE ATTACK has no EDM-style drop. Instead the same order is built into the entry to Chorus 3. The beat section in bars 63–66 is the build-up, the break on bar 67 beats 1–3 is the dark cue, and the anticipation on bar 67 beat 4 is the downbeat place of the drop. Because Chorus 3 comes in one beat early, the lights too open not on the downbeat but on that beat 4. As decided in Part 2, this moment opens with a white flash, not with a blinder or a strobe.

12.6 Kick-Out Bars and the End of the Song

Even in a bar where the music stops for a moment, you do not turn all the lights off. And for the end of the song, you choose in advance how to turn them off.

If you put a blackout on the one bar where the kick drops out, the people on stage disappear and reappear in the next bar. That one bar is not a bar where people vanish but a bar that stops. You leave the key and one base and stop all the effects (the dark cue of Chapter 10).

Bars 33 and 61 of LOVE ATTACK are those places. Both are the one bar after a chorus ends. The key holds 80% on its own fader, the scene is left at the base only at the dark-cue level of Chapter 10, and every effect stops. On the first beat of the next bar, Verse 2’s Garden sunlight comes in after bar 33, and the bridge’s Lilac backlight after bar 61.

For the end of the song, you choose one of two.

  • Button endingCut to 0 on the last note. If you brighten once to white just before the last note and then cut, the ending is clear. After the cut, softly light the front of the stage during the applause so the artist can be seen taking a bow.
  • Fade-outBring it down for as long as the music dies away (3–5 s or more). If you return to the signature look and then bring it down, the start and end of the song connect.

LOVE ATTACK ends with a fade-out, because the kick drops out at bar 82 and the music dies away until bar 83. It is done in two steps. First, on bar 82 beat 1, change Chorus 3’s look to “Pearl heart · night.” It is the pearl heart the pixels drew in the intro, changed to lilac (40%). Then bring this look down to 0 over the two bars 82–83 (4.3 s, Calculation). The heart that bloomed at the start of the song fades again at the end, and you can see that flow in Scene 12-3.

Heard in the field

“Blackout right after the end?” For a broadcast stage that ends on a single song, a blackout leaves a few seconds over the applause when nothing can be seen. Decide in advance with the director whether to put one more cue after the ending cue for the bow, or to move straight on to the next song.

12.7 [Practice] LOVE ATTACK Starting Values by Section and a Design Comparison

Put the looks, colors and brightness of LOVE ATTACK that you set in Part 2 side by side with this chapter’s starting values, and write a reason for every place that differs.

If you can give a reason, it is design, and if you cannot, it is probably a slip.

Follow along

  1. Copy the sections and energy from the song structure table.
  2. For each section, find the starting look in Table 12-1 and write it down. For the beat section use the “Beat section · dance break” row, and for the end use the “Outro (fade)” row.
  3. Find the band in Table 12-2 from the song’s BPM, and write the dimmer, color and position effect cycles in beats and seconds.
  4. For each energy level, write the number of roles on and the number of effects running at once from Table 12-4.
  5. Copy the look sheet and brightness plan you set in Part 2 into the next column.
  6. Mark the cells that differ from the starting values, and write one line on why each differs.
How to check
  1. For each marked cell, point to which line of the concept sheet (Chapter 7) the reason came from.
  2. If the reason comes from the concept, such as “pastels that spread like a scent,” leave it as it is.
  3. If the reason is “I just did it that way,” go back to the starting value and compare the two in rehearsal.

Try it with your own song
  1. Move the sections of the song you are working on now into the same table, and count the cells you decided differently from the starting values.
  2. If there are more than five, either it is a song with a strong concept, or you decided without looking at the starting values.
  3. Write a reason for each cell to tell which it is.

Key points

  • The kind of section sets the starting look. A verse is key-centered and still, a pre-chorus adds each bar, a chorus uses nearly everything, and a bridge cuts down and brings a new color.
  • The BPM band sets the number of beats in an effect cycle. Position is always slower than the dimmer, and cycles are set in 1, 2, 4 or 8 beats.
  • Use accents like punctuation. A verse kick is the first beat only, and a chorus kick is all four beats.
  • Divide energy by the number of roles on, the speed of effects and the beam width, not by brightness. Rhythm layers are two at most at energy 2–4, and three only at the peak of the song. Energy 5 is a moment, not a section.
  • A build-up stacks, the 1 beat to 1 bar before the drop is emptied, and after opening wide at the drop you stop for one bar to show the shape.
  • A kick-out bar is a bar that stops, not a bar that vanishes. For the end of the song, choose between a button ending and a fade-out.
