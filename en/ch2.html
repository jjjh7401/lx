Chapter 2

Beats and Bars

How to Count a Song’s Time

In this chapter
  • Work out the length of a beat, a bar and a phrase straight from one BPM figure.
  • Tell by ear whether a machine-measured BPM is off by half or by double.
  • Set fades and strobe rates in "beats" instead of "seconds."
Opening scene

Watching a show, you sometimes feel the stage is slightly out of step with the music. The lights are busy but move twice as fast as the music, or the moment a color changes slips a little off the beat. The audience can’t say why, but they feel the awkwardness right away.

Sometimes this mismatch starts with a single number. Effect speed, fade time and where to put a cue all come from the song’s BPM. If that number is written at half or double, every effect runs at half speed or twice as busy. The cues land in the right place, yet the stage dances apart from the song.

So before writing cues, count the song’s beats and bars. Every cue from here on is placed on the beats and bars you counted this way.

2.1 Basic Terms

Beat, bar and phrase are the units that measure a song’s time. This section also gathers the terms we touched on briefly in Chapter 1.

Terms to know
Beat
The single pulse you tap your foot or nod your head to while listening to music.
BPM
(beats per minute): how many beats come in one minute. At 120 BPM there are 120 beats a minute, 2 beats a second.
Bar
A group of beats. Most popular music is in 4/4, with four beats to a bar.
Downbeat
The first beat of a bar, the "one" in "one, two, three, four." Cues are usually placed here.
Phrase
One sentence of music made of four or eight bars. Four bars is a short phrase and eight bars is a long phrase.
Hit
A single moment where the music strikes hard, or the short lighting change you put on that moment.
Cue
One lighting state, plus how to get there (time, order), stored together on the console. A list of cues in order is a cue sheet.

Figure 2-1 draws the relationship between beat, bar and phrase on a ruler. The small ticks are beats, the medium ticks are bars, and the large ticks are four bars.

2.2 Length of One Beat and One Bar

If you know the BPM, you can work out at once how many seconds a beat and a bar last. There is just one formula.

Formula

1 beat (s) = 60 ÷ BPM

In a 4/4 song a bar is 4 beats, so 1 bar = 240 ÷ BPM seconds.

Table 2-1 Length of beats and bars by BPM Calculation
BPM 1 beat 1 bar 4 bars 8 bars
80 0.75 s 3.00 s 12.0 s 24.0 s
100 0.60 s 2.40 s 9.6 s 19.2 s
112 0.54 s 2.14 s 8.6 s 17.1 s
120 0.50 s 2.00 s 8.0 s 16.0 s
128 0.47 s 1.88 s 7.5 s 15.0 s
140 0.43 s 1.71 s 6.9 s 13.7 s
150 0.40 s 1.60 s 6.4 s 12.8 s

It helps to memorize 120 BPM: 1 beat is 0.5 s, 1 bar is 2 s, and 8 bars is 16 s. For other songs, get a feel for whether they are faster or slower than 120. Around 120 is also the pace people follow most comfortably. In music psychology research, the tempo people preferred most was 120–125 BPM, about two beats a second. It is close to the pace of repeated body movements such as walking or clapping.1

This book’s practice song, LOVE ATTACK, is 112 BPM. One beat is about 0.536 s and one bar is about 2.143 s. Most examples from here on use these numbers.

2.3 Length of a Phrase

Four beats make a bar, and four or eight bars make a phrase. A song’s sections usually change where a phrase ends.

  • 4 barsA short phrase. One line of lyrics is usually about this long.
  • 8 barsA long phrase and the basic length of a section. Verses and choruses are often 8 or 16 bars.
  • 16 and 32 barsThe build-ups and drops of club music and EDM mostly move in these units (Figure 3-1 in Chapter 3).

Lighting uses these units as they are. Chapter 11 goes into detail; here is just the principle. Match brightness to the beat, moving-light positions to the bar, changes of movement and color to 2–4 bars, and a color set (palette) to the section. For example, brightness rises and falls slightly on every beat, and the moving lights shift to a different position every bar. Over 2–4 bars the color changes little by little, and when the section changes the whole color set is swapped. If you haven’t counted the song in bar numbers, you can’t decide where to put the cues.

Still, 8 bars is only a default. Even in LOVE ATTACK the intro is 6 bars and the first verse is 7 bars. It is not unusual for the vocal to come in a beat early so that the next section starts on beat 4 of the previous bar. Chapter 3 picks this up again.

How to check
  1. At every point where a section changes, write down "which bar is it."
  2. If the numbers you write are 8 apart, like 1, 9, 17, 25, the song is built in 8-bar units.
  3. If even one is off, listen to that spot again.
  4. There is usually a 2-bar breather section or a one-bar rest hiding there.

2.4 Finding the First Downbeat

Knowing the BPM isn’t enough to count beats. You need to find where to start counting: the first downbeat, the place where the first "one" of the song lands.

These sounds usually arrive together on the first downbeat.

  • The kick and a crash cymbal hit together.
  • The bass comes in for the first time.
  • The chord changes.

Many songs also start half a beat or a beat early. If a drum fill goes "ta-da-dak" and then "boom" comes in, the "boom" is the one. Even if the vocal starts singing before the "one," you count the beat from the "boom." A note that comes before the first downbeat like this is called a pickup (in Korean, yeorinnaegi).

Once you find the first downbeat, write down its time. An audio file usually has a short stretch of silence at the front, so the first beat rarely lands at 0.0 s.

LOVE ATTACK is a good example. A beat comes first at 0.96 s, and the first downbeat arrives at 1.50 s. The beat at 0.96 s is the pickup.2

How to check
  1. Count "one, two, three, four" out loud from the first downbeat and listen to the song to the end.
  2. If "one" lands on the first beat of the chorus, you have it right.
  3. If "three" lands there instead, you are off by two beats on the first downbeat.

2.5 BPM That Is Off by Double

Measure the BPM with a machine, then check it by ear. Audio software, DJ software or the tap button on a console will all give you a number, but you shouldn’t trust that number as it is.

The way it goes wrong is fixed. It is off by half or by double. A machine looks for a steadily repeating sound and treats it as the beat. Read a hi-hat that hits every half beat as the beat and you get double; read a snare that hits every two beats as the beat and you get half. In music information retrieval this is called an "octave error," and it is counted among the typical mistakes of tempo and beat tracking programs.3

Consoles also see beats as multiples. grandMA3 has a BPM master that listens to sound and follows the BPM (Speed Master 16). According to the manual, even when you move this fader (the slider you push and pull by hand), the value stops at the nearest multiple of the BPM it hears. On a 60 BPM song, raising the fader to 130 stops it at 120.4 To the machine, 60 and 120 are "different multiples of the same beat." A person has to decide which one is the song’s beat.

Checking by ear is simple.

  1. Clap to the number. If it is too busy to dance to, it is off by double.
  2. Check whether the snare lands on beats 2 and 4. Most pop and K-pop has the snare on 2 and 4. If the snare lands on every beat, the number is probably off by half.
  3. Match it to the speed at which the singer nods their head. That speed is usually the song’s beat.
A short experiment
  1. Set a phone metronome to the BPM you wrote down and play it together with the song.
  2. If the two go together, the number is right.
  3. If the song goes one beat for every two clicks, the number is off by double; if the song goes two beats for every click, it is off by half.

Decimal error. Even a decimal difference builds up and pushes the grid out of line. Feed LOVE ATTACK into an analysis program and it gives 112.35 BPM, but the real figure is 112.0 BPM. The 0.35 difference looks small, but toward the end of the song it drifts almost a full beat. So check the BPM not at one point near the start but by the times of two points far apart. You will try this yourself in the exercise in Section 2.10.

Songs that change tempo. Live band performances, the last chorus of a ballad and a ritardando that slows down gradually have more than one BPM. For these songs, write a separate BPM for each section, or follow that section with tap (Chapter 28).

2.6 Half-time and the Tempo the Body Feels

Sometimes the tempo stays the same but the song suddenly sounds slower. In that case the lights follow the speed that is actually felt, not the number.

For example, some songs are 140 BPM but sound like 70. This is half-time, where the drums play at half speed. The snare lands only on beat 3, not on beats 2 and 4. The tempo is unchanged, but the gap between heavy accents doubles, so the speed the body feels is halved.5

If you run a movement at 140 speed, the stage looks twice as busy as the music. In a section that feels like 70, set the effects to 70 as well.

Table 2-2 Normal beat and half-time (140 BPM)
Normal beat Half-time
Snare 2 and 4 3
Tempo the body feels 140 70
Effect speed Based on 140 Based on 70
Bar length 1.71 s 1.71 s (unchanged)

The important point is that the bar length does not change. Even in half-time, you keep counting the bar numbers the same way. The only thing that changes is "what you feel as the beat in this section."

A song can also move between the two. The chorus runs on the normal beat, then drops heavily into half-time at the dance break (Chapter 3). For songs like this, write the "tempo the body feels" separately for each section.

6/8 and 3/4. Ballads and waltz-type songs divide a bar into threes. A 6/8 song is counted "one-two-three four-five-six," and the body usually feels two big beats. It is natural to set the effect speed to the two big beats as well (Starting value).

2.7 Fades Set in Beats

On the console you write time in seconds, such as a 3-second fade and a 0.5-second delay. But music flows in beats, so when you set a length, start by thinking how many beats it is.

Try applying the same 3-second fade to two songs of different tempo.

  • At 120 BPM, 3 seconds is 6 beats, or 1.5 bars.
  • At 140 BPM, 3 seconds is 7 beats, or 1.75 bars.
Formula

Beats = seconds × BPM ÷ 60

Both of the 3-second fades end in the middle of a bar. At 120 BPM it ends on beat 3 of the second bar, and at 140 BPM on beat 4 of the second bar. The colors should finish changing on the first beat of the next bar, but they end up half-finished in the middle of the bar.

Even the same 3 seconds ends in a different place in each song. Unless a bar happens to be exactly 3 seconds, as in an 80 BPM song, a 3-second fade drifts off the bar.

So decide fades and delays in beats, and write them down converted to seconds.

Formula

Fade (s) = 60 ÷ BPM × beats

Table 2-3 Commonly used lengths at 112 BPM Calculation
Length you want Beats Seconds
Half a beat 0.5 0.27
1 beat 1 0.54
2 beats 2 1.07
1 bar 4 2.14
2 bars 8 4.29
4 bars 16 8.57

On grandMA3 you can give effect speed as BPM and a number of beats (Chapter 25). Put the song’s BPM into a Speed Master and set "how many beats per cycle" for each effect, and when the song changes you only need to change one BPM. There are 16 Speed Masters, and you can set the value between 0 and 225 BPM.4 Fade times are still often written in seconds, so keep a table like Table 2-3 next to the cue sheet.

2.8 Flash Rate and Safety Limits

For a strobe or a flicker (an effect that flashes in a trembling way), the speed is set the moment you choose which note value to flash on. And this speed has a safety limit you must not exceed.

Flash rate is measured in flashes per second, and the unit is Hz (hertz). 3 Hz is three times a second.

Formula

Hz = BPM ÷ 60 × flashes per beat

One flash per beat (quarter note) gives BPM ÷ 60, one every half beat (eighth note) gives double that, and one every quarter beat (sixteenth note) gives four times that.

Table 2-4 Flash rate by BPM and note value Calculation
BPM Quarter note (every beat) Eighth note (every half beat) Sixteenth note
80 1.3 Hz 2.7 Hz 5.3 Hz
90 1.5 Hz 3.0 Hz 6.0 Hz
100 1.7 Hz 3.3 Hz 6.7 Hz
112 1.9 Hz 3.7 Hz 7.5 Hz
120 2.0 Hz 4.0 Hz 8.0 Hz
128 2.1 Hz 4.3 Hz 8.5 Hz
140 2.3 Hz 4.7 Hz 9.3 Hz

The reason to keep to these numbers lies in the audience’s bodies. Flashing light can trigger seizures in people with photosensitive epilepsy. According to the UK’s Epilepsy Society, the light most likely to trigger seizures flashes 3 to 30 times a second. Some people can be sensitive up to 60 Hz, and below 3 Hz it is said to be rare.6 About 3% of people with epilepsy are sensitive to light.7

So there are two standards.

  • Broadcast: 3 times a second. The UK’s Office of Communications (Ofcom) broadcast guidance and the International Telecommunication Union’s recommendation (ITU-R BT.1702) treat a scene as risky when an area larger than 25% of the screen flashes more than 3 times a second. Here, "one flash" is one pair of getting bright and getting dark. If a strobe fires once, that is one flash. Scenes that switch to a highly saturated red are treated as a separate hazard, regardless of brightness.8
  • Venues: 4 times a second. The UK Health and Safety Executive (HSE) recommends a maximum of 4 Hz for strobes in clubs and public events.7 This book uses this 4 Hz as the venue standard.

Two numbers you can use straight from the table.

  • An eighth-note flash at 90 BPM is 3 times a second, right at the edge of the broadcast limit. A faster song goes over it. If you put an eighth-note strobe on fixtures that cover a large part of the screen, it falls foul of the broadcast limit. A value that only just touches the limit is not treated as safe either; use one step slower (Chapter 17).
  • An eighth note at 120 BPM is exactly 4 Hz. In a song faster than 120, an eighth-note strobe also goes over the venue limit.

At the time of writing, I could not confirm Korean broadcasters’ flash standards from public sources.

Chapters 17 and 32 cover broadcast screen limits and venue safety in detail. The one thing to take away here is that choosing a note value fixes the Hz.

2.9 Choosing Hits

You don’t hang a hit on every drum strike; you pick out just a few spots.

Counting a song in bars, you see places to hang a cue everywhere: kicks, snares, claps, shouts. If you hang them all, the stage flashes without a pause. The audience is tired of the flashing before they ever feel the beat.

Moody, whom we met in Chapter 1, described the same scene. He saw a show where the lights flashed on every drum beat and guitar riff, and it was so dizzying that the best moment was the final blackout.9

The criterion for thinning them out is the energy of the section.

Table 2-5 Hit interval by section Starting value
Section Hit interval
Quiet sections (intro, early verse) One every 2–3 beats
Normal sections (verse, pre-chorus) One every 2 beats
Impact sections (first bar of the chorus, drop) Every beat

Only when the places that get a hit on every beat are few across the whole song does that spot burst out. Chapter 10, "Contrast and Restraint," says more about this.

2.10 [Exercise] Bar Time Table for LOVE ATTACK

Take one song and work out for yourself what second each bar starts at, then check it. You need only two things: the BPM and the time of the first downbeat.

Formula

Start time of bar n = first downbeat + (n − 1) × length of 1 bar

Example: LOVE ATTACK, 112 BPM, first downbeat 1.50 s

1 bar = 240 ÷ 112 = 2.143 s

The BPM was found from the big hits of Chorus 1 and Chorus 2. From 37.97 s to 97.97 s there are 112 beats, exactly 60.0 s. At 112 beats a minute, it is 112.0 BPM.2

Whether this grid is right is checked with separately measured moments. I picked four moments that can be clearly caught in the song: the big hits of Chorus 1 and 2, the hit of Chorus 3 that comes in a beat early (Chapter 3), and the downbeat of bar 82 at the end of the song.

Table 2-6 Calculated times and measured times · Measured times are from 2
Spot Measured time Calculated at 112 BPM Difference Calculated at 112.35 BPM Difference
Chorus 1 big hit (bar 18 beat 1) 37.97 s 37.93 s +0.04 37.82 s +0.15
Chorus 2 big hit (bar 46 beat 1) 97.97 s 97.93 s +0.04 97.63 s +0.34
Chorus 3 entry hit (bar 67 beat 4) 144.57 s 144.54 s +0.03 144.09 s +0.48
End-of-song downbeat (bar 82 beat 1) 175.06 s 175.07 s −0.01 174.53 s +0.53

The grid drawn at 112 BPM stays small and constant, within 0.04 s all the way to bar 82. At 112.35 BPM the difference grows toward the end and reaches 0.53 s at bar 82.

The BPM was found between the two hits (bars 18 and 46), so of course those two lines agree. What matters is that it also agrees with the Chorus 3 hit and bar 82, which were not used in that calculation. This confirms 112 BPM.

The first downbeat at 1.50 s is a little different. A grid that fits means the "beats" fit; it does not prove which beat is the "one." The evidence is that the big hits of Chorus 1 and 2 land on the first beat of this grid, but the final check is done by ear (Chapter 5 marks this with ◐). The +0.04 s left over at the hits is a small difference from the measuring method, and you can’t tell it by ear.2

Follow along

  1. Write down the BPM and check it by ear with the method in Section 2.5.
  2. Find the time of the first downbeat.
  3. Use the formula to calculate the times of spots such as bars 1, 9, 17 and 33.
  4. Play the song and listen for whether "one" really lands at those times. If it also fits at bars toward the end of the song, both the BPM and the first beat are right.
  5. If it is off, look at the shape of the drift (Figure 2-6).

The 112.35 column in Table 2-6 is the case in the left figure. The 112 column has a very small, constant difference, so you can use the grid as it is.

This ruler becomes the first line of the song map in Chapter 5.

Try it with your own song
  1. Write down the BPM and first downbeat of your song, and measure the time of one clearly audible hit toward the end of the song.
  2. Compare it with the time calculated by the formula. If the difference is small and constant, the grid is right.

Key points

  • 1 beat = 60 ÷ BPM seconds and 1 bar = 240 ÷ BPM seconds. Memorize 120 BPM (1 beat 0.5 s, 1 bar 2 s) as a reference point.
  • A machine-measured BPM is easily off by half or double. Check it with clapping, the snare position and a metronome.
  • In half-time sections, set effect speed by the tempo the body feels, but keep counting bar numbers the same way.
  • Decide fades in beats and write them down converted to seconds.
  • Choosing a note value fixes the flash Hz. Broadcast must not exceed 3 times a second (3 Hz, over 25% of the screen area) and venues must not exceed 4 times a second (4 Hz).
  • Thin out the hits. The fewer the places that get a hit on every beat, the more those places burst out.
On the job tomorrow
  1. Check the BPM of a song you received today once with a metronome.
  2. Pick one fade written in seconds on a cue sheet and write "how many beats" next to it.
