Chapter 15

Adjusting for Genre

From ballads to EDM

In this chapter
  • Use genre not as the answer but as an adjustment that moves the starting values of Chapter 12.
  • Compare the starting values of ten genres in palette, movement, rhythm layers and accents.
  • Correct the starting values three times, in the order genre → concept → venue.
Opening scene

You have been given a ballad stage. It is a slow song, so you lay down blue and purple and hold the movers still. At rehearsal you hear the lyrics properly for the first time. It is a song about a confession that was put off for a long time on a spring afternoon. The stage stands as cold as a midwinter night.

The genre was right, but the song was wrong. “It’s a ballad, so blue” may be a starting point, but it cannot be a conclusion. In Chapter 7 you read the concept from the sound first, then the emotion, the lyrics and the artist’s concept, in that order. Genre is only one part of that first layer, the sound. This chapter is about how far to trust genre, and where to start following the song.

15.1 Five Things That Change with Genre

Genre is not a rule to keep. It is only a guide for nudging the starting values of Chapter 12 up or down.

In this book the values you move according to genre are called adjustments. There are five.

  • TempoSets the effect cycle (Table 12-2 in Chapter 12). Each genre has a typical BPM range.
  • DensityThe number of rhythm layers running at once, and how tightly the cues are packed.
  • SaturationWhether the palette leans pastel or primary (Chapter 8).
  • MovementHow big the movers move, and how often.
  • AccentWhich of the accent means of Chapter 13 you mainly use.

Genre tells you the first value of these five adjustments. After that, the song decides. Even for two ballads, a song about a breakup and a song about a confession may have their saturation adjustments on opposite sides.

15.2 Starting Values for Ten Genres

Comparing the ten genres often handled on domestic music stages by the five adjustments gives Table 15-1. The values in the table are only a place to start from with a song you have just been given, and they differ from song to song even within the same genre.

Read the table down the columns and you see two things. First, even with the same typical BPM, the adjustments differ. Medium dance pop and city pop overlap in BPM range but differ in rhythm layers and saturation. Second, in no genre is the key among the adjustments. As long as there is a vocal, the key keeps the same level whatever the genre (Chapter 6).

A short experiment
  1. Find one ballad and one dance song sung by the same artist, and watch 30 seconds of each stage video with the sound off.
  2. Write down which of the five adjustments differed most between the two stages.
  3. Usually movement and density differ more than tempo does.

15.3 The Song Comes Before the Genre

If the values picked by genre do not fit the song’s concept, follow the song.

For the ballad in the opening scene, genre says blue and purple, but the lyrics speak of a spring afternoon. If you had read emotion and lyrics after the sound, in the order of the concept layers in Chapter 7, you would have moved the adjustments toward warm white and pale amber.

LOVE ATTACK is the same. By genre it is medium dance pop, and because the drums burst in the chorus, you could also have picked the primary colors and strobes of performance dance. But the one-line concept set in Part 2 was “pastels that spread like a scent, and one last heart.” So the saturation adjustment sits on the pastel side, and instead of strobes it uses a white flash only once (Chapter 10).

When you do not follow the genre, write the reason in the concept sheet. When the directing side or the artist side asks, “It’s a dance song, so why no strobes?”, one line in the concept sheet is the answer.

15.4 What to Fix First at Each Venue

The values set by genre and song are corrected once more to fit the venue where you will perform.

The same song has a different stage on a music show, at a concert, at an awards show, at a festival and in a light show. Part 4 covers each venue in detail; here only the items to fix are gathered.

Table 15-2 What to fix first at each venue Starting value 1
Venue What to fix first Reason
Music show Keep flashes within the standard, fix the key color temperature The camera watches the stage, and each broadcaster has a flash standard (the Ofcom example in Chapter 10; check each broadcaster’s own domestic standard, Chapter 17)
Concert Give each song a different signature look, spread accents over the whole show Several songs run together, so repetition between songs becomes a weakness (Chapter 14)
Awards show Share fixtures between stages, keep transition cues short Several teams share the same fixtures, and the gap between stages is short
Festival A stage designed by someone else, start with lower density You have to build it in a short time on a stage you are seeing for the first time
Light show Raise density, put accents closer together The light, not a person, is the lead
Heard in the field

“It’s TV this time, so please take out the strobes.” If you take the strobes out of a performance dance song, first decide what will fill that place. If you swap in a white bump on each beat or one bar of white, you can mark the same moment within the flash standard.

15.5 [Practice] Correct the Starting Values Three Times

Pick one song and refine the values in the order genre → concept → venue.

If you write down what changed and why each time, you can always trace where the final value came from.

Follow along

  1. Decide the song’s genre and copy that row from Table 15-1. If it is between two genres, write both rows.
  2. With the concept sheet (Chapter 7) beside you, correct whichever of the five adjustments clashes with the concept. For each cell you correct, write which line of the concept sheet it was for.
  3. Decide the venue where the stage will stand, and apply what to fix first from Table 15-2.
  4. Move the values corrected three times into the section table of Chapter 12 (Table 12-5).
How to check
  1. For each cell of the final values, mark where it came from: “genre,” “concept” or “venue.”
  2. If most cells carry only “genre,” you may not yet have read the song enough.
  3. Go back to the concept layers of Chapter 7 and read the emotion and lyrics again.

Try it with your own song
  1. Build the same table for the song you are working on now.
  2. If every “Corrected for concept” cell says “Unchanged,” ask whether that song’s concept truly follows the genre as it is, or whether you have not set a concept yet.

Key points

  • Genre is not a rule but an adjustment that moves the starting values of Chapter 12. There are five: tempo, density, saturation, movement and accent.
  • Ten genres differ in their adjustments even when their typical BPM overlaps. The key is not among the adjustments in any genre.
  • When genre and concept clash, follow the song and write the reason in the concept sheet.
  • What to fix first differs by venue. For a music show it is flashes and the key, for a concert repetition between songs, for a festival density.
  • Correct the starting values three times, in the order genre → concept → venue, and keep the reasons for the corrections.
On the job tomorrow
  1. In today’s song, find one color you picked because “this is that genre,” and pick again from the layer of the lyrics.
  2. If the same color comes out, leave it; if a different color comes out, compare the two in rehearsal.
