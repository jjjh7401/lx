Chapter 8

Color Planning

Palette, white and area

In this chapter
  • Carry a song’s emotion into a palette of one main color and one or two sub colors.
  • Cut the colors visible in one scene down to two, and set aside the places for white and for saturated primaries.
  • Set the key’s white by color temperature, and write other colors as a name and numbers, so that anyone at the console gets the same color.
Opening scene

On a stage packed with LED bars, if you run three or four mixed colors through them, what looked like a rainbow on the console monitor can look like a hazy, muddy lump from the back of the audience. Each small cell of the bar is a deep color, but from far away the neighboring lights overlap.

Using many colors doesn’t make a stage gorgeous. Color becomes clear when you use less of it and give each color a place. This chapter is as much about cutting down colors as about choosing them.

8.1 Emotion and Palette

A palette is a list of colors chosen in advance for one song. You pick one main color that fits the song’s emotion and add one or two sub colors to make it.

In the third listen of Chapter 1 you wrote down the color that came to mind for each section. If you used all of them, one song would end up with seven or eight colors. A palette keeps only the colors that represent this song.

Table 8-1 Makeup of a palette Starting value
Slot Count What it does Example
Main color 1 The base of the whole song. Stays on wash and wide surfaces for a long time Sunset amber
Sub color 1~2 Changes the character of a section Night blue, purple
Special color 0~1 A saturated primary brought out in only one scene of the song (Section 8.4) Red
White By rule Key, white reset, moving beams, and the white flash as a special cue (Section 8.3) Key white by color temperature

Pairings of emotion and color are only a starting point. Like Moody’s pairings in Chapter 1, it is easy to picture yellow and orange for a bright, happy song and blue and lavender for a sad one. If you follow them as they are, every ballad turns blue. If the lyrics and music video (Chapter 7) say a different color, follow that. Putting blue under a ballad that sings of a spring-day confession would be a color chosen for the genre rather than the emotion.

Decide the relation between main and sub color as one of two. Contrast, colors far apart on the color wheel (blue and yellow, red and cyan), is crisp and strong. Neighboring colors, colors close together (pink and lilac, sky blue and cyan), are soft and bind into one mood. If you decide once for each song which it is, you won’t waver when choosing colors for each section.

8.2 How Many Colors Show in One Scene

Don’t let more than two colors show at once in one scene (Starting value). One is the base color spread over the whole scene and the other is the sub color that draws the eye.

With three, the audience can hardly tell which color is the color of the scene, and with four the stage looks like a color swatch chart.

What you count here are the colors of the surfaces the lights paint and of the beams. The key’s white isn’t counted, because the light on a person’s face is not light that paints the scene. Conversely, if the LED wall takes up a wide part of the scene, count the wall’s main color as one too. If the screen is pink, the lights can use pink and only one more color.

You can keep to this rule even with a palette of three or four colors. The palette is the list of colors used across the whole song, and the colors visible at once are the ones drawn from that list for a single scene. If you draw two for each scene, such as main color and sub color 1 for the verse and sub color 2 and main color for the chorus, you use three colors in the whole song while only two show in any scene.

Formula

Set the palette by name, and draw two for each scene.

There are exceptions. Scenes that deliberately spread many colors at once, like a finale or a festive song, and concerts where the audience’s light sticks change color along with the stage, are examples. When you use an exception, do it in that one scene only, and the scenes before and after must return to two colors for that scene to look special.

How to check
  1. Pause one chorus scene in a past performance video and count the colors you see on screen.
  2. If there are three or more apart from the light on faces, think about whether the scene gets clearer if you turn one of them into the base color.

8.3 Where to Use White

Don’t leave white spread under everything; use it only in the places you decided (Starting value). When white flashes once on a stage filled with color, the audience feels that something has begun.

If white is already under everything from the start, that power is lost. There are four places to use white.

  • KeyThe white that lights faces. You don’t mix it from colors but set it by color temperature (see the box below). Fix it to one color temperature for the whole song and don’t mix it with the base wash.
  • White resetWhen the scene changes, passing white briefly at low brightness wipes the color of the previous scene and lets the new color come in clean. Don’t blast it to full brightness; settle it into the new color within about 2 beats (Starting value).
  • Moving beamsWhen showing the path a beam draws, take out the color and keep it white. When the path is the main thing, color actually blurs the line (Chapter 9, Solo, act 1). When the beams stop and paint the scene, they return to the palette’s colors.
  • White flashBlasting the whole stage with full-brightness white on a big hit. Unlike the reset, it is a special cue used only once or twice in a song. The chorus build-up in Chapter 3 saves this for Chorus 3 (Chapter 10).

Some songs have white itself as the concept: songs with white costumes and a white stage creating a clear impression, or a finale that covers the whole stage in white. In that case, write white as the palette’s main color and change scenes with one sub color.

Terms to know — color temperature
Color temperature (K)
A value in kelvin (K) showing how warm or cool a white is. The lower the number, the yellower (tungsten lighting is about 3200 K); the higher, the bluer (midday sunlight is about 5600 K).
CTO · CTB
Corrections that make white warmer (toward orange) or cooler (toward blue). Used as filters on tungsten fixtures and as a color temperature channel on LED fixtures.
White balance
The camera setting that decides which light it treats as white. In broadcast it is usually matched to the key. Then the key becomes neutral white on screen, light with a higher color temperature than the key looks bluer, and light with a lower one looks yellower.
Color rendering (CRI · TLCI)
A value showing how correctly colors appear under that light. CRI is rated against the human eye and TLCI against a broadcast camera.4
White mixed from RGB
A white made by mixing only red, green and blue has gaps in the light’s spectrum, so skin tones tend to look dull. Make the key with fixtures that have a white emitter or with a color temperature channel.

On a stage with cameras, this difference shows up on the picture as it is. An amber wash that looked warm on stage comes out yellower on screen, and a beam that looked cool comes out bluer. That is why you set the key’s color temperature together with the camera side (the meeting table in Chapter 7). Chapter 17 covers the detailed broadcast conditions.

People’s skin and costumes also look different depending on the color of the light, because an object shows only the part of the light reaching it that it reflects. Shine green light on a red garment and the garment looks dark.1 Cyan and green lights easily make skin look pale. White clothes tend to blow out to pure white on screen under the key, black clothes get buried in the background without a back and side, and sequins flash at every angle where they catch light. That is why you ask about the color and material of costumes in the Chapter 7 meeting.

8.4 Pastels and Primaries

Use pastels over wide areas for a long time, and primaries in narrow places for a short time.

Pastels, with their pale (low saturation) color, don’t tire the eye even when spread wide for a long time, and they go well with people’s faces. A deep primary stays in memory strongly when seen once, but looking at it for long tires the eye and makes the next color look weak.

So when choosing a color, decide the area and time along with the color name.

Table 8-2 Where a color goes, set by area and time Starting value
Short (1 beat–1 bar) Long (section)
Wide (wash) White flash Main color (pastel)
Narrow (beams) Special color, snap Key white, accent beam

Use primaries sparingly, as you would a special cue. If you decide the moment a deep red first comes out in the song, that moment becomes the one scene the song is remembered by. Saving things is covered again in Chapter 10.

8.5 Colors That Mix on a Pixel Bar

Run only two colors at a time on a pixel bar. If you run several colors, from far away the colors mix and look hazy.

Light mixing with light is called additive mixing. In additive mixing, the more colors you mix, the lower the saturation and the closer to white, and mixing two colors opposite each other on the color wheel gives white.1

On stage this mixing happens more easily than you would expect. When the cells are small and tightly packed, at the back of the audience the eye or camera cannot tell the cells apart. Light spreads around a bright cell, and in haze it scatters and overlaps with the light of neighboring cells. If the chase is fast, the earlier color and the later color also look mixed. So a bar that alternates red, yellow, green and blue loses the saturation of each color from far away and clumps into a hazy color close to white.

There are two fixes.

  1. Use only two colors. Build the pixel bar’s color effect as two steps alternating two colors. With two colors from the palette (for example blue and purple), even if they look mixed from far away they stay as colors of the same family.
  2. Remove the time colors take to mix. A slow fade between two colors creates a middle color. In a scene where you want clarity, use a snap that changes straight away without a fade (the Color category of the resource library effect dictionary).

In scenes where the color snaps on every beat, also look at the safety standards in Chapter 2. A change to a highly saturated red is treated as a separate hazard regardless of brightness. Scenes where LED bars or beams form stripes also have their own standards in broadcast (Section 2.8 in Chapter 2, and Chapter 17).5

Before and after the fix

An example to aid understanding.

Before A four-color chase of red, yellow, green and blue ran through the LED bars for the whole chorus. From the back of the audience the whole bar looks like a hazy lump.

After Only two colors from the palette remained in the same place, changed with a snap on each beat. The bar reads as a clear line following the stage structure. [Author to confirm]

8.6 How to Write Colors Down

Write the chosen color as a name and numbers together. If you write only “pink”, every person at the console and every fixture gives a different pink.

If you can’t recreate tomorrow the color you chose in rehearsal, you can’t keep the concept either. Copy the numbers exactly as the console uses them. If the console uses RGB in percent, write the values of red, green and blue in order, and add the name the team will call it beside them. Only the key’s white is written not as RGB but as a color temperature (K).

Table 8-3 Example color chips — names and numbers Starting value
Name Red % Green % Blue % Slot
Sunset amber 100 55 10 Main color
Night blue 10 25 100 Sub color
Key white — — — By color temp. (e.g. 4200 K, matched to camera)

Even with the same RGB values, the actual color differs a little depending on the fixture’s LED makeup (RGBW, RGBL and so on) and its color calibration.1 So after writing the values, switch the fixtures on side by side on stage and match them by eye. If there is a camera, check on the monitor too. Attach the matched result straight to the preset name (Chapter 24).

8.7 [Exercise] LOVE ATTACK Palette

Cut the colors in your listening notes down to a palette.

Follow along

  1. Lay out all the colors you wrote for each section in the third listen. Group colors of the same family into one.
  2. Read the Chapter 7 concept layers and one-line concept, and pick the one color closest to the emotion and artist concept as the main color. Also look at the sound-and-lighting starting values in Table 7-2 of Chapter 7.
  3. Decide whether to contrast with the main color or bind it with neighboring colors, and pick one or two sub colors.
  4. Write the white rule. Set the key’s white by color temperature, and decide one place to bring out the white flash.
  5. If you decide on a special color, decide only one, and write the bar of the one scene where you will bring it out.
  6. For each section of the song structure table, write the two colors that will show at once, in the order base color, sub color. Check that no section ends up with three once you leave out the light on faces.
  7. Write the chosen colors as a name and RGB values.

How to check
  1. Look at your palette by three criteria.
  2. Whether two colors show in each scene, whether white is spread under everything, and whether the special color appears in only one scene.
  3. Then compare with the author’s example.
  4. The author swapped the magenta and purple that came to mind while listening for pearl pink and lilac, neighboring colors, and dropped amber and cyan to settle on one aqua.
  5. The bridge, where blue came to mind, was moved to lilac.
  6. Aqua first shows in the pre-chorus and becomes the base color in the chorus, and the deep red is used only in the one scene of the pixel heart inside Chorus 2.
  7. If you chose different colors, that is fine as long as the three criteria hold.
Try it with your own song
  1. Make a palette for the song you are in charge of now and write two colors for each section.
  2. Put it beside the LED wall colors you got from the video team (Chapter 7) and find the sections that overlap.

Key points

  • Build a palette from one main color, one or two sub colors, one special color if needed, and a white rule.
  • Pairings of emotion and color are a starting point. Follow the colors the lyrics and music video say over the genre.
  • Two colors show at once in a scene. The color of a widely visible LED wall counts too. A scene that deliberately spreads many colors, like a finale, is an exception used once.
  • White goes to the key, the white reset, moving beams, and the white flash as a special cue. If white itself is the concept, write it as the main color.
  • Set the key’s white by color temperature, not by mixing RGB, and when there is a camera, set it together with the white balance.
  • Use pastels wide and long, primaries narrow and short.
  • The more light mixes, the blurrier it gets. On pixel bars, use two colors from the palette with a snap.
