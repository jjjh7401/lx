Chapter 13

Building Layers

Splitting the light by instrument

In this chapter
  • Compare two ways of splitting scenes and rhythm layers into several sequences, and choose one.
  • Assign one group to each instrument and carry the layers of the music over to layers of light.
  • Know in advance the cases where values on the same fixture overwrite each other, and build the placement chart for LOVE ATTACK’s 83 bars.
Opening scene

On the first day of rehearsal, the director says, “Make the Chorus 2 entry a little bigger.” But the cue list has no separate entry for Chorus 2. The scene color, the back pulse, the pixel snap and the movers’ position are all packed into one cue per bar. To change the entry, you have to open the cues one by one from bar 46 to bar 60.

The next day you receive a version of the track with a changed BPM. To fix the speed of the pulse, you now have to open every cue in the whole song.

The problem was not the number of cues. It was that jobs of different character were tied into one line of cue. In Chapter 11 you saw the principle of putting rhythm layers on top of a scene. This chapter covers how to keep those layers separate on the console.

13.1 When Everything Goes into One Cue

If you put the scene and the rhythm layers all into one cue, it is quick to build at first, but it takes a lot of hands to fix.

It is easy to make, since you are taking one snapshot of the whole stage per bar. The trouble comes when you fix things.

  • When fixingTo change one back pulse, you have to open every cue that contains that pulse.
  • When changing speedIf pulse, chase and moving are tied in the same cue, you cannot speed up just one.
  • When switching off and onEven when someone in rehearsal says, “Let’s try it without the pixels,” you cannot turn off just the pixels.

So jobs of different character go into separate sequences. The scene goes in the scene sequence, and the back pulse in the pulse sequence. With several sequences, each can be fixed separately, sped up separately and turned off separately.

Terms to know
Cue
A saved picture of the stage at one moment. Press GO and you move to the next cue.
Sequence
A list of cues bound in order, also called a cue list. On the console, you attach one executor (a play button or fader) to each sequence and run them separately.
Executor
The button or fader that plays a sequence. It is the term used on grandMA3; other consoles sometimes call it a playback.

13.2 Two Ways to Split Sequences

You split sequences by directing role, or by fixture type (a family of fixtures). Which is right differs from show to show.

Table 13-1 Two ways to split sequences
By role By family
What you split by Scene, back pulse, side chase, pixel, upper movers, lower movers, accent Ceiling movers, tower movers, floor beams, pixel, strobe
Relation to the music One layer of the music is one sequence One layer of the music may be scattered across several sequences
Good points “Let’s try it without the pulse” is one button. You change the speed of each layer separately Fixtures sit in only one sequence, so values rarely overwrite each other. Easy to rebuild on a tour where the fixture layout changes
Watch out for The same fixture goes into several sequences, so you must split things so two sequences do not touch the same attribute (Section 13.5) To fix one layer of the music, you have to open several sequences

The practice in this book splits by role. The beat grid of Chapter 11 is built from role rows, and one layer of the music matches one sequence, so it is easy to read. Shows that split by family come up again in the case studies of Part 7.[Author to confirm]

Whichever way you choose, the key stays outside the song’s sequences. While the song goes on, the key must stay at the same level whatever the song cues do. If you put it on a separate sequence or a fixed fader, the key will not go out while you are fixing song cues (Chapter 6).

13.3 One Group per Instrument

When you split rhythm layers into sequences, you pair one group with one instrument.

If the kick jumps on the back and also on the sides, the audience cannot find the kick on stage. If the kick jumps only on the floor beams, the audience looks at that spot every time the kick sounds. Just as Chapter 6 gave each fixture a role, this time you give each role an instrument.

Giving the hi-hat to the sides and the claps to the pixels runs in the same direction as the starting values of Table 12-3 in Chapter 12. The hi-hat is grouped by beat and taken as a left-right alternating chase on the sides, and the claps are taken as a two-color snap on the pixels, a narrow surface.

The kick on the floor beams is missing from Verse 1 and Choruses 1 and 2 in the table so as to keep rhythm layers to two (Chapter 11). In Verse 1, the side’s hi-hat and the movers are already two layers, and in Choruses 1 and 2, the back’s low bass and the pixel’s claps are two layers. The kick jumping on every beat on the floor beams happens only in Chorus 3, where the layers become three. In Verse 2, instead of adding the kick on the first beat of the bar, the movers are held still.

As decided in Part 2, LOVE ATTACK uses no strobes. In the author’s first design notes the claps were taken by a strobe, but to fit the concept of “pastels that spread like a scent,” they were changed to the pixels’ two-color snap (Chapter 10).

13.4 One Accent per Cue

Each time the chorus returns you may add a layer, but you bring out only one means of changing the stage dramatically at a time (Section 11.3).

Here, any means that changes the stage greatly for a moment, including the entry means that marks the moment of stepping into a section, is called an accent. There are seven accent means.

Table 13-3 Seven accent means and LOVE ATTACK 1

Strobe Fast flicker Not used
Audience Lights the house (blinders, audience beams) Not used

If you stack two or more accents in one cue, none of them looks big. If you use a white flash and a mover burst on the same downbeat, the audience remembers only a stage washed in white. So you use one accent per cue, and switch to a different means the next round. Brightness alone is the exception, built up round by round (80%, 90%, 100%).

Bar 78 of LOVE ATTACK is not an accent but the peak of roles on. From this bar of Chorus 3 every role of the fixtures is on, but the rhythm layers stay at three and no new accent means is brought out. Count the roles on (Table 12-4 in Chapter 12) and the rhythm layers (Chapter 11) separately.

13.5 When Values on the Same Fixture Overwrite Each Other

Once you split into several sequences, there are moments when two sequences send different values to the same attribute of the same fixture.

Then only one value remains. Which value remains is decided by the HTP and LTP rules you saw in Chapter 11. The person splitting the sequences should know this rule and prevent values from overwriting each other in advance.

First, the scene’s dimmer hides the pulse. This is the case you saw in Section 11.7. You do not put the dimmer of the fixtures that carry the pulse in the scene sequence. The rule is one sequence takes one attribute of one fixture.

Second, a narrow group’s fixed value comes over a wide group’s effect. Say you have put a left-right alternating chase on all of the sides (8), and in another cue you give the left 4 sides a fixed level: the effect drops out on those 4. Only the other 4 flicker, and the chase becomes half. When you give a fixed value to part of a group that has an effect on it, check that it is intended.

Third, a fixed value covers only some steps of an effect. On some consoles an effect is stored in several steps. If you put the fixed value in the first step only, the remaining steps of the previous cue’s effect stay, and in a scene that should be still the dimmer flickers or the movers keep moving.

Before and after the fix

Before After I moved one song onto the console and checked the values per fixture against each cue, I found steps of the previous cue’s effect remaining in 177 places in scenes that should have been fixed. The dimmer went back and forth between 100% and 0%, or the movers kept moving in a fixed position.1

After I refilled the presets holding fixed values with the same value up to the largest number of steps of the effects used in that song, and saved them again. The leftover steps dropped to 0 places.1 A check that only counts cues and whether presets exist cannot find this. The concrete method on the console is covered in Part 5.

13.6 [Practice] A Placement Chart for LOVE ATTACK’s 83 Bars

Extend the beat grid you drew for bars 1–25 in Chapter 11 to all 83 bars of the song, and attach one sequence to each role row.

When it is drawn, you can see at a glance which layer comes on and goes off when across the whole song.

Follow along

  1. As in Table 13-2, choose one group for each instrument and give each group a sequence name (scene, back low bass, floor beam kick, side hi-hat, pixel claps, upper movers, lower movers, accent). Keep the key separate.
  2. Write the attribute each sequence takes. If the same attribute of the same fixture is in two sequences, tidy it into one.
  3. Draw a horizontal line for bars 1–83 and shade the sections where each sequence is on.
  4. On the bottom row, write the number of rhythm layers running at once for each bar. Count a bar where the upper and lower movers are merged into the same movement as one. Check that the place with three is one peak of the song.
  5. On the “Accent” row, mark the accents of Table 13-3. Check that no place has two or more accents stacked on one downbeat.
How to check
  1. In the finished chart, cover one sequence with your hand and run the song through in your head.
  2. If the flow of the song stays the same without that sequence, that layer may be one that does no work.
  3. Conversely, if covering one leaves a section empty, that section leans too much on one layer.

The scene sequence holds the movers’ base position, and the upper and lower mover sequences add only movement on top of it as relative values (Chapter 11). So even if two sequences touch the same movers’ position, they do not overwrite each other.

Try it with your own song
  1. In the song you are working on now, pick four instruments, attach one group to each, and draw the same placement chart.
  2. Count the bars with three or more layers, and if there are more than the peak of the song, drop one layer or move it to another section.

In Web tool 11, Beat Grid, choose the “Whole song” view and you can see all 83 bars on one screen.

Key points

  • If you tie jobs of different character into one cue, fixing one opens the rest as well. Split scenes and rhythm layers into separate sequences.
  • Sequences are split by directing role or by fixture type. By role is easy to match to the music, and by family has fewer overwritten values.
  • The key stays outside the song’s sequences.
  • Assign one group to one instrument, so the audience finds that instrument on stage.
  • Layers may pile up round by round, but use one accent per cue and switch to a different means the next round.
  • One sequence takes one attribute of one fixture. Check whether a scene’s dimmer hides a pulse, and whether a fixed value covers only some steps of an effect.
On the job tomorrow
  1. In today’s show, pick one layer someone is likely to say “let’s try it without this one” about, and check that you can turn off just that layer.
  2. If you cannot, split that layer off into one sequence.
