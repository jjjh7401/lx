Chapter 14

Transitions

Connecting one scene to the next

In this chapter
  • Tell eight transition tools apart by the shape of their brightness curve: snap, fade, dip then hit, and the rest.
  • Cut the attributes you change greatly in one transition down to one or two, and change the rest in the dark.
  • Plan round-by-round build-ups for repeated sections, and fill in a transition table for the eleven boundaries of LOVE ATTACK.
Opening scene

Picture the moment in a concert when one song ends and the next begins. With the last note the whole stage turns white, and about two beats later that white settles and a color you have never seen before rises. The audience knows the song has changed without being told.

Some stages handle the same moment differently. The color changes, the movers move, the beam size changes and the effect changes, all at once. The stage changed busily, but the audience cannot say what changed.

The difference between the two stages is not the amount that changed but the order and the method of changing. If Chapters 11–13 settled the work inside a section, this chapter deals with the work between sections.

14.1 Eight Transition Tools

The ways of changing a scene fall into eight kinds, according to the shape the brightness takes as it changes.

In Chapter 9 you wrote an “in time” for each look. If the in time is “how long it takes,” the transition tool is “in what shape.” Even for the same two beats, a plain fade and a drop followed by a hit make entirely different transitions.

Table 14-1 Eight transition tools Starting value 1
Transition tool Shape of the brightness Length Where it fits
Snap Changes all at once on the downbeat 0 beats Start of the song, chorus entry, a beat section where the music comes in with a cut, a button ending
Fade Changes evenly n beats (60 ÷ BPM × n seconds) Verse entry, bridge
Dip then hit Drops for 1–4 beats, then hits on the downbeat 1–4 beats + 0 Big chorus entry, drop
White reset Low-level white passes briefly and settles into the new color (Chapter 8) About 2 beats Between songs, the last chorus
Bump Rises briefly, then returns to where it was Around 1 beat End of a vocal line, a crash
Swell The wash slowly swells 1–2 bars Pre-chorus, build-up
Freeze Stops the effect and leaves it as it is 0 beats Kick-out bar, a freeze in the choreography
Crossfade The color spreads and changes over a long time Around 10 s Ballad, long intro and outro

The white reset is different from the white flash of Chapter 10. A white flash is a one-time flare for emphasis; a white reset uses that white to erase the previous scene and then raises the new one.

There is one rule about length: make transitions where energy rises short, and those where it falls long. A chorus entry is a snap, and coming down from the chorus to a verse is a fade, because settling an emotion takes time. As starting values, use 1.5–2 s for a section change (about 3–4 beats at 112 BPM), 3–5 s for a build-up or a change of emotion, and 5 s or more for a ballad.1 When the bar before is one that stops, like a kick-out bar, that bar stands in for the time it takes to come down, so the fade into the next section can be as short as 1–2 beats (bars 33–34 in Table 14-3).

Terms to know — the timing of a snap
Snap
is a transition with a fade time of zero. But if you press GO after hearing the downbeat, you are late by your reaction time.

14.2 Change Only One or Two Attributes Greatly

If you change many things at once when changing a scene, the audience cannot tell what changed.

When the five attributes of Chapter 6 (dimmer, color, position, beam, timing) and the effect all change at once, the audience knows only that something changed. If just one or two change greatly, the audience reads them. The white reset in the opening scene was clear because only two things changed greatly, dimmer and color.

That does not mean you should leave the rest unchanged. Choose the one or two to change greatly, and change the rest when the audience is not looking. The way to do that is MIB (Move In Black) in the next section.

A short experiment
  1. In a concert video you like, pick out only the moments where the song changes and watch five of them.
  2. Each time, write down in one word what changed (“color,” “beams go up,” “gets dark”). The easier a transition is to write in one word, the more clearly it stayed in memory.

14.3 Changing Things Where the Audience Cannot See (MIB)

Change the direction of the moving lights and the color of the scene, as far as possible, while the light is off. On the job this is called MIB (Move In Black).

If the movers travel to their next position with the light on, the audience sees beams sweeping across the stage. If that is the intent, it makes a good scene, but if it is not, the stage looks unfinished.

The starting values are these.1

  • When moving without being seenMove during the dark cue before a hit (Chapter 11) or in a kick-out bar. The console has a function that moves to the next position ahead of time, before the dimmer rises from 0. On grandMA3 you set this with the MIB setting of each cue.2
  • When moving so it is seenTo show the movement itself, move with the light on over 1–1.5 beats so that the arrival falls on the next beat. The mover burst is this case.
  • When showing a big move slowlyMove over about 10 s. Anything shorter leaves only the look of a sweep.
  • ColorIf you also change the scene’s color in the dark, the new color appears all at once when the light comes on. If you must change it with the light on, make it short (0.15–0.35 s) so that no in-between color is seen.

In LOVE ATTACK, bar 17 beats 3–4 is that place. During the two beats of the dark cue, the movers go from the wall to the fan, and the scene’s base goes from pearl pink to aqua. What the audience sees on bar 18 beat 1 is a single brightness hit. In fact three things changed, including position and color, but to the audience’s eye it reads as one: “it got a lot brighter.”

14.4 Adding One Thing at a Time in Repeated Sections

When the same section comes back, as a chorus does, use the look you used before as it is, and add one new accent at the moment of stepping in.

In this book this is called a round-by-round build-up. It is the method Chapter 3 used for the chorus, applied to the verse and pre-chorus too. If you change Chorus 2 into a completely different look from Chorus 1, the audience will not recognize it as the chorus. Repetition inside a song is a device that lets the audience follow the song.

Table 14-2 LOVE ATTACK round-by-round build-up — the author’s example [Author to confirm]
Repeated section First round Next round Last round
Verse Verse 1: Garden sunlight, 50% Verse 2: + floor beam kick pulse, 60% —
Pre-chorus Pre 1: Balloon sky, 70% Pre 2: + dark cue on bars 42–43, 75% —
Chorus Chorus 1: Rooftop fan, 80%, brightness hit Chorus 2: + mover burst, red pixels on bars 47–48, 90% Chorus 3: Rooftop fan · night, + white flash, kick layer, 100%

The rule is the same as in Section 13.4. The entry accent brought out each round is one. If you add both the mover burst and the white flash to Chorus 2, nothing is left to bring out in Chorus 3. The red heart in Chorus 2 is not an entry accent but the special color planned separately in Chapter 10, and the kick layer and the “night” color in Chorus 3 open layers and a look variation together at the peak of the song.

14.5 Between Songs

Within a song, a look coming back is a strength; between songs, it is a weakness.

If the chorus of the previous song and the chorus of the next share the same color and position, the audience remembers the two songs as one. So in a show that runs several songs together, you set a different signature look for each song (Chapter 9) and put one transition cue between songs.

A transition cue does three things.

  1. Clear: Stop every effect that was running in the previous song and return the sequence to the start. This prevents the accident of the previous song’s pulse remaining in the next song’s intro.
  2. Leave some light: Do not make the stage completely dark; leave it faint. The artists and crew must be able to move to their next positions.
  3. Prepare the next song: In the meantime, move the position and color of the next song’s first look in the dark.

The white reset in the opening scene is a way of doing all three at once. The white erases the colors of the previous song, and while that white settles, the colors of the next song rise.

14.6 [Practice] A Transition Table for the Eleven Section Changes in LOVE ATTACK

Find every place in the song where the section changes, and write on one sheet how you will change it at each.

You write four things: the transition tool, the in time, the attribute that changes greatly, and what you change in the dark. When it is filled in, the transitions of the whole song can be seen at a glance.

Follow along

  1. From the song structure table, pull out every section boundary. Count the start and the end of the song as boundaries too.
  2. At each boundary, mark whether the energy rises or falls. The starting value is short if it rises and long if it falls.
  3. Choose the transition tool from Table 14-1, and write the in time in beats and seconds.
  4. Choose the one or two attributes to change greatly. Of the rest, write whatever must change in the “in the dark” column, and write when you will move it (dark cue, kick-out bar).
  5. For the boundaries of repeated sections, check that they match the round-by-round build-up of Table 14-2.
How to check
  1. In the finished table, look for rows where three or more attributes change greatly.
  2. In such a row, see whether you can move one of them into the dark.
  3. If there is no dark cue to move it into, listen to the music again and see whether you can empty one beat before that boundary.

Beat → Chorus 3 67.4 Rises Dip then hit (white flash) 3 beats + 0 Dimmer, color (white) Position (fan), scene color (aqua · lilac): bar 67 beats 1–3
Chorus 3 → End 82 Falls Fade 2 bars, 4.3 s Dimmer, color (lilac) —

Of the eleven boundaries, none has a row where three attributes change greatly. At big boundaries like bars 18, 46 and 67.4, there is a dark cue or kick-out before them, so the rest can be moved in between. The music has already made the room.

Try it with your own song
  1. Pull out the boundaries of the song you are working on now and build the same table.
  2. Pick one boundary where three or more attributes change greatly, and in rehearsal run only that boundary two ways (as it is, and with one attribute moved into the dark) and compare them.

Key points

  • Transition tools are told apart by the shape of the brightness. There are eight: snap, fade, dip then hit, white reset, bump, swell, freeze and crossfade.
  • Make transitions where energy rises short, and those where it falls long.
  • Change only one or two attributes greatly in one transition. Change the rest in the dark, in a dark cue or a kick-out bar.
  • To show a mover’s travel, move it with the light on over 1–1.5 beats; to hide it, move it in the dark with MIB.
  • A repeated section gets the same look plus one new entry accent (round-by-round build-up). Brightness is stacked; accents are swapped.
  • Repetition within a song is a strength; repetition between songs is a weakness. Between songs, put a transition cue that clears, leaves some light and prepares the next song.
On the job tomorrow
  1. In today’s cue list, find one transition where the movers travel a long way with the light on, and move that travel into the dark cue before it.
  2. See whether what changes on the downbeat becomes clearer.
