Chapter 22

Lighting Design and the Rig

From the song to the lighting plot and patch sheet

In this chapter
  • Work through lighting design in this order: song, artist and venue → looks → roles → fixtures → positions and quantities → lighting plot → patch sheet.
  • Pull the roles you need out of the look sheet, and for each role decide the type of fixture, its position and its quantity.
  • Make the lighting plot and patch sheet of the practice stage and hand them to the console, the simulation, and the rigging and electrical crews.
Opening scene

Put two concert photos side by side. In one, dozens of beams rise into the sky from towers behind the stage; in the other, a soft light falling from overhead wraps the whole stage.

Say the moving lights in the two photos are the same model. Then what made the two stages look different is not the equipment but how many were hung where, and pointed which way. And what decides those places is the music that will be played on the stage. A stage where light streaks shoot into the sky is a design for a song whose main is the beams, and a stage that wraps from overhead is a design for a song whose main is the singing and the face.

At a concert or a music show the lighting director sometimes receives a stage designed by someone else, but often designs the stage himself or herself.[Author to confirm] You talk with the artist’s team, listen to the songs and choose the equipment. Then you draw the lighting plot, make the patch sheet and hand them to the console. In this chapter we follow that process from the song through to the end. The weight is on the order in which you choose equipment to suit the song rather than on the specifications of each fixture.

22.1 From the Song to the Patch Sheet

Lighting design starts not from an equipment list but from the song. From looking at the song to making the patch sheet takes seven steps.

If you pick equipment first, you end up worrying about “what shall I do with this equipment?”; if you look at the song first, you worry about “what does this song need?”

  1. Understand the song, artist and venue: the concept sheet and look sheet made through Part 2, and the conditions of the stage (Table 22-1).
  2. Find the roles the looks need: gather the main role of each look, and decide the direction and place of light that the song needs (Section 22.2).
  3. Choose fixtures: choose the type of fixture for each role (Section 22.3).
  4. Position, angle, quantity: set each fixture’s position according to its role, and calculate the quantity from the beam diameter (Section 22.4).
  5. Lighting plot: draw where each fixture hangs and which way it points (Section 22.5).
  6. Patch sheet: arrange the console number (ID) and DMX address for each fixture (Section 22.6).
  7. Check and hand over: check the load and power, and hand the documents to the people in charge (Section 22.7).

When we trimmed the tour rig in Chapter 18 we said “keep the role, cut the quantity.” That is possible because the role of step 2 comes before the quantity of step 4.

What to ask the artist’s team is in the concept meeting of Chapter 7 (Table 7-4). Ask about the conditions of the stage to the stage manager, rigging, electrical and camera sides.

On a music show the stage design and the basic rig are set by the broadcaster and the period (Section 17.2), and on a stage made for one song, like a comeback stage, equipment is sometimes added to suit that song.[Author to confirm] Either way, the starting point of the design is the same: the looks of the song that will be on that stage.

Read the table backwards and you can see why the practice rig of Chapter 6 was put together the way it was. There are two sections where beams are the main — the chorus and the beat section — and the fan has to be drawn in two kinds, narrow and wide. That is why there are the most moving beams. Conversely, there are roles that do not appear in this song’s look sheet. LOVE ATTACK uses neither strobes nor audience lights (Table 13-3, Chapter 13). Even so, the practice rig has 8 strobes and 4 blinders because this stage is used not for one song but for several.

When there are several songs, lay the look sheets on top of one another. A role that appears repeatedly across many songs of the set list (Chapter 18) is treated as base equipment, and a role that appears in only one or two songs as equipment added for that song. If equipment for one song is expensive or heavy, first see whether the same picture can be made with the base equipment. Handling a different song every week by cues on a music show (Section 17.2) is the same idea. You cannot change equipment for each song, so the base equipment takes the roles common to many songs.

More equipment does not mean a better design. The equipment of a role that does not appear in the look sheet is equipment that rests in that song.

22.3 Choosing Fixtures

Once you have gathered the roles, now choose what type of fixture to use for each role. Before that, decide which roles to have on this stage at all.

Which roles are needed differs from venue to venue. On a music show the key cannot be left out, but a light show with no people on stage has no key (Chapter 20). So instead of a fixed list, answer the questions of Table 22-3 for each role, and check them against the roles you gathered in Table 22-2.

When choosing a model within the same type, look at the beam angle and zoom range (whether the diameter you want comes out at the hanging distance, Section 22.4), brightness, the color system (whether it can make the pastels and deep red of the Chapter 8 palette), and, for a key, color rendering and color temperature (Section 17.4). Channel count, weight and power are used in the universes and the load and power calculations (Sections 22.6–22.7), so copy them from the spec sheet at the same time.

If the role is the same, the model can be swapped. If the rental company doesn’t have the same model, change to one with a similar beam angle, brightness and color system, and fix only that line of the plot and patch sheet. Conversely, even with the same model, a different role means different equipment. The same moving wash becomes a back on the back truss and a side on the side tower.

More equipment does not mean it suits every stage. A stage with no people, like the light show of Chapter 20, has no key, fill or side even when it has more than twice the fixtures of the practice rig; most of it is beams and pixels. If you played a stage with people on that rig, before adding equipment you would have to decide where the key comes from.1

22.4 Position, Angle, Quantity

Once you have chosen the type of fixture, decide where, at what angle and how many to hang. You decide the position by looking at the role, and the quantity by looking at the size of the circle one fixture lights.

Position is decided by the role. That is because which direction the light comes from is exactly the fixture’s role.

  • KeyFrom about 45 degrees front-above. Divide the stage into zones and light each zone from two directions, left and right, crossing over (Chapter 6).3
  • FillLower and weaker than the key, from close to the front.
  • BackFrom behind and above. It comes down steeper than the key in front.
  • SideFrom both sides of the stage, close to horizontal. The low layer lights the outline of the body at the height from knee to shoulder, and the high layer lights obliquely from above (Starting value).
  • TopDirectly overhead.
  • Beam, pixelPlaces seen from the audience. Usually behind the stage, to the sides, and on the floor.

Quantity is calculated from the beam diameter. The diameter of the circle one fixture lights is set by the beam angle and the distance. The beam angle is the value written on the fixture’s spec sheet: the angle between the two directions where the brightness falls to 50% of the center.4 The spec sheet also lists the field angle, out to the point where it falls to 10%, but this book uses the beam angle for the calculation of even coverage (Starting value).

Formula

Beam diameter = 2 × distance × tan(beam angle ÷ 2)

If the distance from the fixture to the lit surface is 10 m and the beam angle is 20°, the diameter is 2 × 10 × tan 10° ≈ 3.5 m.

22.5 The Lighting Plot

The lighting plot is a drawing that shows where each fixture hangs and which way it points. Several people look at one drawing, each for a different purpose.

The rigging crew uses it to check the trusses and the load, the systems crew to check DMX lines and cables, and the programmer to check the fixtures’ coordinates.

The plot needs four things.

  1. Plan: the view from above. The front-back and left-right places of trusses and fixtures.
  2. Elevation: the view from the audience. The heights of fixtures and the levels of the towers.
  3. Legend: the symbol is the type of fixture and the color is the role. Next to each symbol, the fixture number (ID).
  4. Position table: the names and heights of the trusses and towers and the fixtures hung on them.

There are four rules for drawing.

  • Set the origin and axes the same as the console. X 0 is the center of the stage and +X is stage left (the right as seen from the audience), Y 0 is the front edge of the stage and +Y is upstage, and Z 0 is the stage floor (Section 24.2). The numbers on the plot become the console’s coordinates as they are.
  • Every fixture hangs somewhere. It is one of a truss, a tower, the floor or the set. If there is a fixture floating in the air, the plot is wrong.
  • Give each position a name. A name such as “audience truss” or “back tower SR” becomes the position column of the patch sheet and the name of a sub-group on the console.
  • Within the same position, number from stage right (the left as seen from the audience). It is the same as the numbering rule of Chapter 24. In a position name, SR is stage right and SL is stage left.

On site the lighting plot is usually drawn in Vectorworks.10 You keep the fixture information in one drawing, and the patch sheet, the console and the simulation program all use that data.

  1. Plot: place trusses and fixtures in Vectorworks, and enter a number, universe and address, and position for each fixture.
  2. Patch sheet: make the patch sheet from the fixture information entered in the plot. Because the plot and the patch sheet are linked, when you fix one you can bring the other into line.10
  3. MVR export: when you export the plot as an MVR file, the console (grandMA3) and the simulation programs (Vision, Capture, Depence and others) receive the same fixture layout.2 9
  4. Merging the revised layout back into the plot: a layout changed in the simulation program can be merged back into the plot.9

The flow of previewing and programming with a simulation is covered in Part 5 (Chapter 23, Section 28.5).

22.6 The Patch Sheet

The patch sheet is a table that arranges, for each fixture, the console number (ID), mode and DMX address (universe.address). If the plot shows where a fixture is, the patch sheet says what number it is on the console and what address.

You can write fixtures one by one, or group them by type or position and write only the ID range and the start address (Table 22-10). To make it, either link it with the plot in Vectorworks (Section 22.5) or write it separately in a spreadsheet. ID: fixture numbers (fixture IDs) are assigned by dividing number ranges by type (in the 100s, the 200s and so on). For the practice rig, LED profiles are 101–108, LED Fresnels 121–126, moving washes 201–212, side tower washes 221–228, moving spots 301–308, moving beams 401–416, LED bars 501–524, strobes 601–608 and blinders 701–704 (Table 24-1, Chapter 24). From the number alone you can tell which fixture it is, and if you add more of the same type later the numbers do not get mixed up.

Channel count is not guessed. Copy it for each mode from the fixture’s spec sheet or the console’s channel map (Section 24.1). For the onPC practice you can use a similar model. But when you patch real equipment, if you put in a different model that merely looks similar because the model is not in the console library, the fixtures will move wrongly on the first day of rehearsal.

Universes: one DMX universe holds 512 channels, and you cannot split one fixture across two universes. There are three ways to divide universes.

  • ChainingYou put fixtures into addresses in order and, when 512 channels are full, move on to the next universe. There are few empty channels, so it uses the fewest universes. For the practice rig the channels add up to 2,040. Divided by 512 it looks as if 4 would do, but if you fill them in the order of Table 22-11 you need 5. That is because when there is not enough room for the next fixture, the remaining channels are left empty and you move on to the next universe.
  • By typeStart a new universe each time the fixture type changes. You can tell the fixture from the universe number alone, but the universes multiply. The practice rig needs 10.
  • By DMX lineUse a separate universe for each place on stage where a signal cable runs. The positions on the plot and the universes match, so it is easy to lay cables and to find a place that has failed.

The practice stage is divided by DMX line, and within one universe it chains the same type, but when the type changes it starts at the next hundred address (101, 201…) (Starting value). From the address alone you can tell which type it is.

An LED bar has 30 channels, so up to 17 fit in one universe. So the 8 fixtures of level 3 were moved to universe 7. The spare universe (8) is the place to move things to when fixtures are added on site or one DMX line fails.

When the patch sheet is written, check five things.

  1. No IDs overlap.
  2. No fixture overlaps or goes past 512. A fixture’s start address + channel count − 1 must be within 512.
  3. Every fixture has a position and X, Y, Z. Every fixture on the plot must be in the patch sheet, and the reverse as well.
  4. The quantities and channel total match the fixture list. If the number of lines in the patch sheet differs from the quantities in the fixture list, a fixture has been missed or entered twice.
  5. There is a spare universe or spare addresses.

Three levels of groups: the position and role columns of the patch sheet become the console’s groups as they are. Build groups in three levels: all-group → sub-group → effect group.1

  1. All-group: a whole type. “All beams,” “all LED bars.”
  2. Sub-group: divided by position on the plot. “Back tower SR,” “stairs level 1,” left and right, front and back.
  3. Effect group: split for the direction and order of an effect. Odd/even, left → right, bottom → top, checker.

Effect groups are made from other groups, so make the all-groups and sub-groups first. As on the tour in Chapter 18, even if the quantities change from venue to venue, if the names and levels of the groups are the same, the cue structure is used as it is. How to make groups on the console is covered in Section 24.3.

22.7 Load, Power and the Documents to Hand Over

When the plot and patch sheet are done, check the weight the trusses can bear and the venue’s power, and hand the documents over, divided by recipient. If you get stuck on weight or power, go back to an earlier step and fix the quantities or positions.

Load: add up the weight of the fixtures hung on one truss, and divide by the number of hoists that hang the truss to see the load per hoist. On the practice stage’s back truss, 6 moving washes (132 kg) and 8 strobes (96 kg) are hung, so the fixtures alone are 228 kg, and with 4 hoists that is 57 kg each (Calculation). Add the weight of the truss itself and the cables. The actual safe load follows the manufacturer’s specifications for the truss and hoists and the check of the venue’s rigging crew. Safety and regulations are covered in Part 6 (Section 32.5).

Power: add up each fixture’s power consumption. The 94 fixtures of the practice rig (excluding the 2 follow spots) total 42.1 kW (Table 22-11, Calculation). On a tour the power available differs from venue to venue, so also calculate the power of the trimmed rig (Chapter 18).

Documents to hand over: when the design is done, hand the documents over, divided by who receives them.

  • Rigging crew, stage managerThe lighting plot, the position table, the load per truss.
  • Electrical crewThe power total and the power per position.
  • Systems crewThe DMX lines and universe plan of the patch sheet.
  • Programmer, simulation operatorThe patch sheet and the plot file (MVR). They are the material for starting programming on the console and in the simulation (the workflow of Chapter 23). The receiving side first checks that the number and places of fixtures match the patch sheet and plot (Section 24.7).

If even one fixture changes, fix the four documents (lighting plot, position table, load and power table, patch sheet) together and hand them over again. If you fix only the plot and leave the patch sheet as it is, the console’s coordinates and the actual places of the fixtures drift apart.

If you receive a stage someone else designed, you walk this order backwards. From the plot and patch sheet you received, re-divide the fixtures by role, and fill the empty roles first. That order is in Table 21-2 of Chapter 21.

  4. Check that LED bars 517–524 moved as a block to universe 7.

  6. Finally, check that for each main role of Table 22-2 there is a fixture on the plot to take it, and that no fixture is floating in the air.
Try it with your own song
  1. Gather the main roles from the look sheet of the song or set list you are working on now, and find the roles missing from your current rig and the fixtures that rest.
  2. If there are several songs, separate the roles that appear in many songs from those that appear in only one, and make a list of base equipment and equipment to add.

Key points

  • Lighting design proceeds in this order: song, artist and venue → looks → roles → fixtures → positions and quantities → lighting plot → patch sheet. Roles come before fixtures.
  • Gather the main roles of the look sheet and the equipment you need comes out. Roles that appear across many songs are base equipment, and roles that appear in only one song are equipment added for that song.
  • Which roles to have differs from venue to venue, so ask what to check for each role and decide by matching against the look sheet. If the role is the same, the model can be swapped.
  • Decide position by role, and calculate the quantities of key and wash from the beam diameter (2 × distance × tan(beam angle ÷ 2)). Decide the quantity of beams by looking at the shape you will draw.
  • The lighting plot is the plan, elevation, legend and position table. Set the origin and axes the same as the console, and draw it so that every fixture hangs somewhere.
  • The patch sheet is a table that arranges ID, mode and universe.address for each fixture. Do not split a fixture at a universe boundary, keep a spare universe, and check the five points.
  • If the equipment changes, fix the plot, position table, load and power table and patch sheet together and hand them over again.
On the job tomorrow
  1. Open the patch sheet of the stage you are using now and check whether each fixture has a position and X, Y, Z written in.
  2. If it is empty, fill in the position names first, matching them to the plot.
  3. Those names become the names of the sub-groups as they are.
Closing Part 4

In Part 4 we saw how the same song changes from venue to venue.

On a music show the camera came first (Chapter 17), at a concert the flow of the whole set list (Chapter 18), at an awards show the space between parts (Chapter 19), in a light show the solos of the equipment (Chapter 20), and at a festival improvisation (Chapter 21). And when you design the stage yourself, the starting point was still the looks of the song (Chapter 22).

The principles of Parts 1–3 held as they were at every venue. Keep the key separate. One main per scene. Save the peak. What changed from venue to venue was not the principles but the unit at which they were applied.
