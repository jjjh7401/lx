Opening scene

Listen to the same song ten times and you hear something new ten times. At first you hear only the chorus, then the spot where the bass drops out, and then even the hi-hat that hits half a beat early. There is no end to what you could write down.

So decide first where to stop. There are two criteria: the time you have left, and the job the lights have to do in that song. Gather what you heard onto one sheet, and choose how detailed to be to fit the time.

5.1 What the Song Map Does

The song map gathers what you heard in Chapters 1–4 (beats, sections, energy, changes) onto one sheet. Once you have made it, from then on you work from the sheet instead of listening to the song again.

A song map is used in three main ways.

  • When building cuesAs you make the cue sheet (Chapter 16), you decide where the cues go from the song map alone, without listening to the song again from the start.
  • When working togetherWith the director, the console programmer and the audio engineer, you can point at the same spot by bar number, as in "the bar 18 chorus hit."
  • When taking it up againEven if you take the same song months later or receive a remix version, you carry on from the song map instead of listening from scratch.

In all three cases, anyone should be able to find the same spot whenever they look. That is why the song map uses bar numbers, not seconds (Chapter 1), and marks what you checked yourself separately from what you guessed (Section 5.4).

5.2 The One-Hour Basic Version

When prep time is short, write only enough to decide where to put the cues. These are the six cells on the left of Figure 5-1.

Table 5-1 Order and time of the basic version — for a 3-minute song Starting value
Order Task Chapter Time
1 Listen three times (mainly passes 1 and 2) Chapter 1 15 min
2 Check the BPM by ear and find the first downbeat Chapter 2 10 min
3 Write section start bars, names and pass numbers Chapter 3 15 min
4 Write impacts (▼ ◆), kick-outs (×) and the energy 5 spots Chapter 4 10 min
5 Check the grid on bars late in the song and mark certainty Chapter 2 · Section 5.4 10 min

It adds up to one hour. If you have only 30 minutes, cut the third listen and the energy verdicts, but don’t cut the BPM and first downbeat (step 2) or the section map (step 3). If the BPM, first downbeat or section map is wrong, every cue laid on top of them goes out of line.

What is often left out of the basic version is the time the music really ends.

The length of the audio file and the length of the music can differ. The LOVE ATTACK audio file is 197 seconds, but the music ends at about 179 seconds and the last 18 seconds are almost silent.1 If you place the last cue by the file length alone, the stage waits for 18 seconds with no sound.

5.3 The Detailed Version When You Have Time

If you have plenty of prep time, add four cells to the basic version (the right side of Figure 5-1).

  • All energyWrite 1–5 for every section, not just the big spots (Figure 4-2 in Chapter 4).
  • All changesWrite not only the impacts but also points such as the ▲ entry and transitions such as the ↗ build-up, ≋ snare roll and ○ break.
  • Rhythm layersDivide one chorus bar into 16 cells and write where the kick, snare, hi-hat and bass land (Section 4.5).
  • Color notesWrite the colors that came to mind in the third listen for each section (Section 1.2).

The detailed version is needed when you use many cues on one song, when you put the same song on stage many times, and when you link several songs in one flow. The rhythm layers and color notes are used as they are in Chapter 13 (Layering) and Chapter 8 (Color Planning).

5.4 What Is Certain and What Is a Guess

Not everything written in the song map is equally trustworthy. So mark how certain each thing you wrote is.

Table 5-2 Three certainty marks
Mark Meaning LOVE ATTACK example
● Measured Measured, or checked by ear several times 112 BPM (112 beats between two hits = 60 s, grid fits through bar 82), the times of the chorus entries and kick-out spots
◐ Guessed There is evidence but it is not settled First downbeat 1.50 s (three pieces of evidence agree but final confirmation by ear is needed, so bar numbers like 18 and 46 follow this judgment too), the name "pre-chorus" for bars 14–17
○ Undecided Two or more answers remain Whether the "beat drop" critics talk about falls at bars 63–66 or at bars 18 and 46

1

With these marks, you know what to check first at rehearsal. Listen to the ○ first, then the ◐. You don’t need to recheck the ●.

Results from an automatic analysis program are all treated as ◐ at first. Figure 5-2 puts side by side how an automatic analysis program the author tried divided LOVE ATTACK, and the result checked by listening and counting bars by hand.

The program got the positions of the three choruses and the big flow right. But all 8 boundaries that can be compared with bars were placed about a beat early (0.41–0.82 s) compared with the real downbeats. The boundary of the verse-2 pre-chorus was three whole bars earlier than the real one.

It called both the build-up and the bridge "verse," and it did not catch the kick-out bars or Chorus 3 coming in a beat early. It gave the 18 seconds of silence at the end of the song a section of its own.1 A tool gets you a starting point quickly, but to raise a mark to ● you have to check it by ear.

The map shows the job of this song’s lights. There are two big hits, at bars 18 and 46, each with a 4-bar build-up before it. A bar of kick drops out at the end of each chorus, and Chorus 3 comes in a beat early and runs to the end of the song. Bar 67, right before it, is the spot that sounds the emptiest when you listen. Try it with your own song
  1. Fill in the basic version for one song you are working on.
  2. Time yourself, and if it takes more than an hour, write down which cell took the longest.
  3. On the next song, cut down from that cell first.

Key points

  • The song map is what you gather onto one sheet before moving what you heard into cues. Write it in bars so that the people you work with can point at the same spot.
  • If time is short, use the basic version (song info, BPM · first beat, section map, key changes, energy 5, certainty); if you have plenty, use the detailed version (all energy and changes, rhythm layers, color notes).
  • Even when you are short of time, don’t cut the BPM · first downbeat and the section map.
  • The length of the audio file and the time the music ends can differ.
  • Mark each thing you wrote as ● measured, ◐ guessed or ○ undecided. The result of an automatic analysis is ◐ until you check it by ear.

End of Part 1

Part 1 covered how to read a song. You listened three times (Chapter 1), counted beats and bars (Chapter 2), divided it into sections and gave them names and numbers (Chapter 3), wrote down energy and change (Chapter 4), and gathered all of it onto one song map (Chapter 5).

Now you hold one song map. In Part 2, "Designing the Light," you use this sheet to decide what to show: with which equipment, with what concept and color, and who to put in front as the main.
