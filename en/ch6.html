Designing the Light

Once you have listened to the whole song, you decide what lighting to show for it. You divide up which job each piece of equipment will take, then decide the mood and color, what to put in front in each scene, and where to hold back brightness. It is the rough sketch you draw before building cues.

Fixtures and Their Roles

Five Things Light Can Change

In this chapter
  • Divide your fixtures not by name but by role (key, fill, back, side, top, wash, beam, FX, pixel, audience).
  • Tell apart the five attributes that light can change on any fixture (dimmer, color, position, beam, time).
  • Put the fixtures you are in charge of onto one role table and find the roles that are empty.
Opening scene

While watching a concert video, stop on one scene. The beams cutting across the screen, the color filling the back of the stage and the beams sweeping the floor make the light look countless. Yet among all that light, only a few lights are on the singer’s face.

The light on the face doesn’t stand out on screen. Still, if it goes out, the stage looks empty at once. So when you look at fixtures, look first at the job each light has, not the quantity. Before deciding in Part 2 what to show, divide the fixtures you have into roles.

6.1 Fixtures Seen by Role

Divide fixtures by the role they take rather than by name. The same moving light becomes a light that brightens the face (key) when it lights the face from the audience side, and a light that picks out a person’s outline (back) when it lights them from behind the stage.

The reverse also happens: several fixtures of different kinds share one role.

Terms to know — roles of lights
Key
The main light that makes the face and body visible. Usually it shines from above and in front, on the audience side.
Fill
A weak light that softens the shadows the key makes.
Back
A light from behind that traces a person’s outline and separates them from the background.
Side
A light from the side that brings out the body’s volume and movement. It matters especially when showing dance.
Top
A light that comes straight down and marks the spot where a person stands.
Wash
A light that covers a wide area of the stage in color. The section’s palette is set here.
Beam
A narrow light that draws lines in the air. It is visible only with haze.
FX
A light that marks a brief moment, like a strobe.
Pixel
A light that turns on each cell separately, like an LED bar or matrix.
Audience
A light that shines toward the audience. Blinders and audience washes take this job.

The ten roles divide into three groups by what they do.

  • Performer lightingKey, fill, back, side, top. They make the face, body and outline of the people on stage.
  • Background lightingWash, pixel. They make the stage’s base color and structure.
  • Effect lightingBeam, FX, audience. They make lines in the air and short flashes, and extend the light out to the audience.

There is one light source that sits outside the ten roles but must be looked at together with them. It is the LED wall. The big screen behind the stage is often the widest and brightest light source on the stage, and it affects both the exposure of faces and the contrast of the background. It is not lighting, so it isn’t put in the role table, but the brighter the screen, the more the key and back must be raised along with it for people to stand apart from the screen (Chapters 7 and 17).

The use becomes clear when you lay it over the sound and light matching of Chapter 1 (Figure 1-2). Melody and people go to the key and beam, weight and harmony go to the wash, rhythm goes to dimmer pulses and pixels, and big impacts go to audience lighting such as blinders.

Among the performer lights, the key is kept as a light that does not go out until the song ends, except for moments such as an intended blackout or silhouette (Starting value). A stagecraft textbook explains that light coming from about 45 degrees above and in front is close to the angle of sunlight and makes faces look natural. The same textbook says that lighting from a single unit straight in front makes the face look flat, so it recommends overlapping two units from the left and right at an angle.3 This 45 degrees is a starting point. If a hat or bangs hide the eyes, lower the angle, and on a stage that goes out to the screen through a camera, check on the monitor whether light reaches the eyes. However gorgeous the background, a dark face is a failure (Chapter 1).

The key is not a problem only when one person stands in one spot. On a group stage where several people move all over the stage, divide the stage into a few zones and light each zone with two or more overlapping keys, so that faces stay lit wherever anyone goes (Starting value).

A short experiment
  1. In a dark room, shine a phone flashlight on your own face in a mirror.
  2. Shine it in turn from front and above, from the side, from behind, and from straight above.
  3. The light from front and above makes the face visible, the light from the side makes volume, the light from behind makes the outline, and the light from straight above makes shadows under the eyes.

6.2 Five Attributes of Light

What light can change on any fixture comes down to five things: brightness (dimmer), color, direction of the beam (position), shape of the beam (beam), and the time it takes to change (time).

This book uses the names you see on the console screen as they are. The first four are names under which the console groups a fixture’s functions, and time is the fade and delay time attached to a cue or an effect. Depending on the console, beam may be shown further divided into gobo, beam and focus.5

Stage lighting textbooks name four properties of light that you can control: intensity, color, distribution and movement. This framework is known to have started from the summary by the 1930s American lighting designer Stanley McCandless.12

This book’s five attributes are these four divided to suit moving lights and consoles. Intensity becomes dimmer and color becomes color. Distribution is split into where the light points (position) and what shape the beam has (beam). Movement is treated as the time light takes to change (time), which includes the speed of fades and effects.

Table 6-1 Where each attribute changes and the music unit it is matched to
Attribute What changes Where it changes on the fixture Music unit to match Starting value Category in the resource library effect dictionary
Dimmer How bright Dimmer channel, strobe shutter Beat Dimmer, Shutter · Strobe, Pixel · Matrix
Color Which color Color mixing (RGB etc.), color wheel, CTO Section (palette), 2–4 bars (color tint) Color, Pixel · Matrix
Position Where it points Pan and tilt, the fixture’s place Bar Pan · Tilt, Static Looks
Beam What shape the beam is Zoom, iris, gobo, prism, focus, frost Section (beam width) Beam, Static Looks
Time How fast it changes Fade · delay, effect speed Set in beats (Chapter 2) Spread and Layout Variants, Music Sync and Timing, Operation · Busking

The "music unit to match" column is a starting value that carries the principle of Section 2.3 in Chapter 2 over to the five attributes. Match dimmer to the beat, position to the bar, the flow of movement and color to 2–4 bars, and palette and beam width to the section, and set time in beats. Chapter 11 covers this in detail.

Once you tell the five apart, you can say in words what a cue changes. As you build cues within the same section, the starting point is to change one thing at a time other than dimmer. It is the same idea as the chorus build in Chapter 3, which said to use only one means at the moment a chorus starts. If a single cue changes two or more things at once within the same section, the audience can hardly tell what changed.

The boundary where a section changes is different. When passing from verse to chorus, it is natural for color, position and beam to change all at once so the whole scene is renewed. Chapter 9 treats this as a change of look.

How to check
  1. Pick one cue inside a section from your last show and count which of the five attributes that cue changes.
  2. If two or more besides dimmer change, consider whether it would be clearer to postpone one of them to the next cue.

6.3 Fixture Types and Beam Shapes

What separates fixture types is beam shape and the attributes they can change. Rather than memorizing fixture names, look at what kind of beam a fixture makes and which of the five attributes it can change.

Table 6-2 Beam shape and the roles each fixture type suits Starting value
Type Beam shape Attributes it can change Roles it suits
Profile (ERS) A circle with a sharp edge; the shape is set by hand with framing shutters Dimmer, color (LED type) Key
Moving profile A sharp circle, with framing shutters moved from the console Dimmer, color, position, beam (shutter · zoom · gobo) Key, beam (pattern)
Fresnel · PAR Wide light with a soft edge Dimmer, color (LED type) Fill, wash, back
Wash moving Wide, soft light, size set by zoom Dimmer, color, position, beam (zoom) Wash, back, side, top
Spot moving A sharp circle, gobo and prism Dimmer, color, position, beam (zoom · gobo · prism) Beam (pattern), key (support)
Beam moving A very narrow beam, building shapes in the air Dimmer, color, position, beam (prism) Beam
Hybrid Spot, beam and wash in one unit, suited to stages with few fixtures Dimmer, color, position, beam (all three shapes) Beam, wash, key (support)
LED bar · pixel A line or surface where each cell lights separately, showing the stage structure Dimmer, color (per cell) Pixel
Strobe A very bright, short flash Dimmer (LED type also color and zone) FX
Blinder Wide, warm light facing the audience Dimmer (LED type also color temperature) Audience
Follow spot A sharp circle that follows a person (some are moved remotely) Dimmer, position, beam (as the operator follows) Key (lead)

The "attributes it can change" column lists what can be changed during the show. A follow spot is changed by a person following. Time (the speed of fades and effects, the flash speed of a strobe) is set on the console, not the fixture, so I left it out of the table.

On big stages today, a moving profile often takes the key from the front. This is because the shape of the light can be trimmed with shutters from the console, and it can be moved to a different person depending on the scene. In broadcast studios, wide, soft LED lights are sometimes placed in front and used as the key.

The table shows representative cases. Even in the same type, zoom range and color method differ by model, and many fixtures fall outside the table, such as LED bars that can tilt, so check actual fixtures against the spec sheet. The flashing of strobes and pixels follows the safety standards in Chapters 2 and 32. Broadcast standards are judged on the output picture, so also keep in mind that even the same strobe takes up a different share of the screen depending on the size of the camera shot (Chapter 17).

One thing to remember about beam shapes is air. A narrow beam is visible only if the air the light passes through has particles. On a stage without haze, a beam moving light is seen only as a dot on the floor or wall. If a section will use the beam as the main, check the haze first (the Special Effects and Other Fixtures category of the resource library effect dictionary).

A short experiment
  1. Pause on one scene of a concert video you like and count the visible lights in three groups.
  2. Lights on people, background lighting, and lights that draw lines in the air.
  3. Write down which group is largest and how many lights are on people.

6.4 Twelve Categories of the Effect Dictionary

The effect dictionary in the resource library divides 153 cards into twelve categories.4 Each category has a main attribute it changes, so if you know the five attributes of Section 6.2, you can find which category to open.

The map shows the categories with the most cards are Pan · Tilt (position), Operation · Busking (time) and Dimmer (brightness). Shutter · Strobe and Special Effects have five cards each. When to use a strobe and when to hold it back is covered in Chapter 10.

Follow along

  1. Write the fixture list as type, quantity and position.
  2. Give each row a role. If it has two roles, write the main role first.
  3. Count the quantity for each role. Find the roles that are empty among the ten, and fill key, back and side first if they are empty.
  4. Write the attributes each row can change. Find rows where the role and attributes don’t match, such as a beam that can’t change position or a wash that can’t change color.
  5. Check whether the fixture taking the key can stay on until the song ends. If it is tied up in an effect, move the key to another fixture.
  6. Divide the stage into a few zones, front-back and left-right, and see whether each zone has two or more overlapping keys. If a place where people walk has only one key, fill that spot.
How to check
  1. Compare with your result for Table 6-4.
  2. The fixtures with key as their main role are 8 profiles and 2 follow spots, 10 units in all.
  3. The 8 spot moving lights take the key as a secondary role, but they can’t light faces while a beam effect is running on them (Follow along step 5). No fixture takes the top role (Follow along step 3). The next question is how many of the wash moving lights on the rear truss can be turned to the top.
  4. Table 6-4 does not list zones, so check Follow along step 6 when you try it with your own stage.
Try it with your own stage
  1. Fill in Table 6-4 with the fixture list of the stage you are in charge of now.
  2. If a role is missing, write which fixture will fill it.

Key points

  • Look at fixtures by role, not name. Performer lighting (key, fill, back, side, top), background lighting (wash, pixel) and effect lighting (beam, FX, audience).
  • Keep the key as a light that does not go out until the song ends, except for moments such as an intended blackout or silhouette. The starting point is light overlapped from left and right at about 45 degrees from front and above; divide the stage into zones and overlap the keys in each zone.
  • What light can change is five things: dimmer, color, position, beam and time. They are the names on the console screen exactly. Within a section, change one thing at a time other than dimmer, and at a section boundary change the whole look.
  • What separates fixture types is beam shape and the attributes they can change. A narrow beam in the beam role is visible only with haze.
  • Find the twelve categories of the effect dictionary by the main attribute they change.
On the job tomorrow
  1. Pick one group on the console that carries only a fixture name, and add the role that group takes to its name.
  2. Check whether the group taking the key stays on all through the song.
