Chapter 10

Contrast and Restraint

Saving intensity and using darkness

In this chapter
  • Learn to leave headroom for the peak of the song.
  • Use darkness as a cue, and decide in advance the special cues you will bring out only once or twice in a song.
  • Gather the decisions of Chapters 7–10 and complete the LOVE ATTACK concept sheet.
Opening scene

When you hear in rehearsal that the stage looks flat, your hand goes to the master fader first. You raise it once, raise it again on the next song, and before you know it every cue is stuck at 100%. And at the moment the song bursts biggest, there is nowhere left to go up.

What looks big is not the brightness itself but the difference before and after. This chapter, the last of Part 2, covers when to save and when to bring out the fixtures, concept, colors and looks you have chosen so far.

10.1 Brightness Saved for the Peak

Don’t raise brightness all the way until the biggest moment of the song arrives. On a stage that is always at 100%, the brightest moment doesn’t stand out.

The eye gets used to brightness quickly, so the brightness an audience feels depends heavily on how different it is from the scene just before. Just as a recording engineer calls the margin kept for the loudest sound headroom, lighting needs headroom too.

Table 10-1 Brightness by place in a section Starting value
Place Main Base Key
Dark (kick-out, break) 0~20% 10~20% Same all song
Energy 1–2 40~60% 20~40% 〃
Energy 3 60~75% 40~50% 〃
Energy 4 80~90% 50~70% 〃
Peak (one section) 100% 70~80% 〃

In a section where the key is the main (a section where the singing is the center, like a verse), read the “Main role” column as the brightness of the role that steps forward after the key (wash or side). The key itself holds the same value all song, as in the rightmost column.

In sections where the same energy repeats, like choruses, raise it each time following the chorus build-up in Chapter 3. For LOVE ATTACK that is 80% for Chorus 1, 90% for Chorus 2 and 100% for Chorus 3. The hits at bars 18 and 46, which Chapter 4 rated energy 5, are also not played at 100%; they burst at the value for that pass. 100% is saved for one section of the song only, the peak.

Even inside that peak section there is something to save once more: the moment all roles are on at once. Since it is the only moment that deliberately breaks “one main per scene” from Chapter 9, you bring it out once in the song (Section 10.3). For LOVE ATTACK that is bar 78 inside Chorus 3.

The key doesn’t rise and fall with energy. If the brightness of the face changes from section to section, both the camera’s exposure and the audience’s gaze waver. What moves with energy is the main and the base. And the one thing that must never happen is the key going down when the energy goes up.

Terms to know — percent and real brightness
Percent is a console value
The percent in the table is the value of the console fader. It is not the same as the brightness the eye feels. The eye feels differences in brightness as ratios, so 80% versus 90% on the bright end looks like a smaller difference than 20% versus 30% on the dark end.
Brightness differs by fixture
Even at the same percent, real brightness differs from fixture to fixture, because each fixture’s output and dimmer curve (the curve that turns a fader value into real brightness) are different. Don’t read beam 80% and wash 80% as the same brightness.
Checking it
So Table 10-1 is a starting value for setting the order between sections. On the real stage, check the difference by eye, and by monitor if there is a camera. If you measure the key with a light meter or the camera’s waveform monitor, you can recreate the same value in every show.

Brightness is not the only thing to leave room in. Ahead of the peak, don’t use brightness, time (the speed of effects) and beam spread all at their maximum together (Starting value). A scene where the beams turn fastest, widest and brightest is enough once in a song.

One number

When the author counted the 5-minute light show script he made, brightness had only two values, 0 and 100: 100 appeared 67 times and 0 appeared 70 times. A light show is a show about the equipment, so it can be written this way, but on a stage with a song the peak would not have been visible.1

10.2 Dark Cues

Briefly emptying the stage into darkness is also a cue. Light that bursts after darkness looks much bigger than light added to an already bright stage.

Half the reason a hit looks big depends on what came right before it. The music already tells you where to use darkness: the breaks (○) and kick-outs (×) you marked in Chapter 4. If the lights empty along with the music at the moment it empties, the audience waits for the next moment.

Table 10-2 Length of darkness Starting value
Place Length Example
A short breath at the end of a section Half a beat to 1 bar One kick-out bar (LOVE ATTACK bar 33)
Right before a hit 1 beat to 1 bar Anticipation after a break (a hit that comes in a beat early) (LOVE ATTACK bar 67 beats 1–3)
Before a drop As long as the music is empty (e.g. about 3 s) After the build-up ends and before the drop lands

Hold the darkness before a drop for as long as the music is empty. If the music stays empty for a long time after the build-up ends, make the darkness long; if short, short. In the light show script the author made, the darkness before the drop was 2.8–3.2 seconds.1 At that show’s tempo of 136 BPM, that is 6–7 beats, a little under two bars. If the darkness is shorter than the music, it doesn’t feel like darkness; if it is longer, the show looks as if it has stopped.

Unlike the short dark cues you use at each place the music empties, darkness that cuts the song in a big way is enough two or three times per song (Starting value).2 If you darken every section, darkness becomes a habit and has no power. And even in the dark, keep the key. On a stage with someone singing, a complete blackout is used only at one or two intended moments.

The moment special effects such as fireworks or CO₂ go off is handled by the same principle. First decide who the main is at that moment. If the special effect is the main, take light away just before, and while the effect goes off don’t trigger a new cue, or support it with the same color. If the lighting hit is the main, suggest moving the effect one beat later. If both burst at maximum on the same beat, it becomes the “everything on” from Chapter 9. Agree the timing and the main with the stage and special effects side in the Chapter 7 concept meeting.

A short experiment
  1. With the room light on, switch on your phone flashlight, then turn the room light off and switch it on again.
  2. It is the same flashlight, but the second time feels much brighter.
  3. That is what darkness before a hit does.

10.3 Special Cues

For strong effects you will use only once or twice in a song, decide in advance when to bring them out. If you show the flashiest effect in the first chorus, there is nothing left to show from the second chorus on.

This is also why the chorus build-up in Chapter 3 switched tools for each chorus. What to save is usually as in Table 10-3. Bring out only one special cue per cue, the same rule as “one of the other means per cue” in Chapter 3.

Table 10-3 Special cues and where they first appear Starting value
Special cue First appears How often
Moving burst Chorus entry 1–2 (1 per chorus)
White flash Last chorus entry 1–2 per song
Strobe The biggest impact 0–2 (Sec. 10.4)
Blinder (audience) Big hit after Chorus 2 2 or fewer
Special color (Chapter 8) One scene In that scene only
Audience beam Last chorus or ending 1 per song
All roles at once The peak of the song 1 per song
Special rig (B stage, kinetics) Song or show peak Once in the show

It is the same when you widen it to the whole show. If you show all the equipment you have in the first song of the setlist, every song left looks smaller than it. Chapter 18 covers a show’s special cues.

10.4 Strobes and the Beat

Don’t keep firing the strobe on the beat. If you fire a strobe at every clap or snare, the first few times look exciting, but soon the stage looks like a blinking clock.

The audience is already hearing the beat. If the lights just count the beat, they add nothing to the music (Chapter 2, choosing hits).

Use the strobe only for real impacts. Those are the places marked ▼ and ◆ in Chapter 4 (Starting value). For LOVE ATTACK the candidates are the two big hits (bars 18 and 46) and the anticipation (bar 67 beat 4). But each of these three places already holds one of the chorus build-up’s tools (brightness hit, moving burst, white flash). Since a cue gets one special cue, this book’s example uses no strobe. Plenty of songs have no strobe at all.

Before and after the fix

Before The design file the author made for this song put a strobe on every clap in the chorus and fired it 107 times in one song.1 The count per second was below the standard, but flashing that continues through a whole chorus is a case that needs separate care even within the standard (below).

After All strobes were removed, and the two-color snap on the pixel bar takes the clap spots. A single white flash is enough for the Chorus 3 entry. [Author to confirm]

When using strobes, also keep to the safety standards in Chapter 2. In broadcast, flashing with large brightness changes is flagged if it covers more than 25% of the screen area and exceeds 3 times per second. A change to a highly saturated red is treated as a separate hazard regardless of brightness, and flashing that continues for more than 5 seconds can be hazardous even within the standard.3 In a venue, as set in Chapter 2, don’t exceed 4 times per second (4 Hz). How wide a strobe appears on screen depends on the camera’s shot size, so in broadcast check it together with the camera side (Chapter 17).

Strobes also don’t have to be white only. For a song with a pastel palette, there is a way to keep the palette by firing the strobe in the pastel of the main color.1

10.5 Eight Common Mistakes

Stages where the lighting looks clumsy share common symptoms. I gather them into eight and use them as a rehearsal checklist.2

Most of them come from not keeping the principles in Part 2.

Table 10-4 Symptoms and remedies of a clumsy-looking stage
Symptom Remedy Covered in
Every cue is the same, only the brightness differs Turn them into looks combining position, color, dimmer level and effect Ch. 9
Colors that change for no reason, too much saturation Set a palette, two colors per scene, primaries short Ch. 8
Movement for the sake of moving Match movement to bars and sections, use position presets Ch. 9, 11
Strobes firing on every beat Only for real impacts Sec. 10.4
White going straight into the audience’s eyes Save audience beams and blinders, keep them short Sec. 10.3, Ch. 32
Using everything the equipment can do One main per scene Ch. 9
Dark performers At least one light per performer, key to the end Ch. 6 (key), 17
No contrast between songs A different representative look and palette for each song Ch. 8, 9, 18

You can use this table as a checklist after rehearsal. If even one of the eight rows applies, go back to that row’s “Covered in” and look at the principle again.

10.6 [Exercise] LOVE ATTACK Concept Sheet

Gather what you decided in Chapters 7 to 10 onto one page. It is the concept sheet that Chapter 7 said to take to the meeting.

Table 10-5 Fields of a concept sheet

Concept layers Sound, emotion, lyrics, artist concept Ch. 7

Motif One repeating shape Ch. 7

Color flow Two colors for each section Ch. 8

Follow along

  1. Copy the Chapter 7 concept layers, one-line concept and the light you won’t use to the top.
  2. Write the Chapter 8 palette as names and RGB values (key white as a color temperature), and write two colors for each section of the song structure table.
  3. From the Chapter 9 look sheet, copy only the look names and mains.
  4. Looking at the Chapter 4 energy and the Chapter 3 chorus build-up, write the main’s brightness for each section. Use 100% in only one peak section of the song.
  5. Write two or three dark cues by bar.
  6. For each special cue, write the bar where it first appears. If two or more special cues crowd into one bar, move one to another bar, and if special cues crowd into Chorus 1, push them later.
  7. At the bottom, write what to ask in the meeting, and check that it doesn’t run past one page.
How to check
  1. Give the finished page to a colleague who doesn’t know the song and have them say what Chorus 3 will look like.
  2. If they get the colors, the main and the brightness roughly right, the page has done its job.
  3. When comparing with the author’s example in Figure 10-4, look not at whether the answers are the same but at whether every field is filled and whether the special cues sit toward the back.
Try it with your own song
  1. Make a concept sheet for the song you are in charge of now and take it to the next meeting.
  2. Write what the other teams fixed in the margin, and after rehearsal compare it with what actually changed.

Key points

  • What looks big is the difference before and after. Use 100% in only one peak section of the song, and don’t use brightness, time and beam spread all at maximum together.
  • The console’s percent is not the brightness the eye feels. The table values are starting values for setting the order; check the real difference by eye and monitor, and the key by light meter.
  • The key doesn’t rise and fall with energy. The key must never go down when energy goes up.
  • Darkness is a cue. Where the music empties (breaks, kick-outs), empty the lights for as long as the music is empty. Two or three times per song is enough.
  • For the moment special effects go off, decide the main first, and don’t let the lights and the effect burst at maximum on the same beat.
  • For the white flash, strobe, blinder, special color and all roles at once, decide the first bar in advance. One special cue per cue.
  • Don’t count the beat with a strobe. Use it only for real impacts, and keep to the safety standards not just in count but in area and duration. Some songs use none.
  • Gather the concept layers, palette, look sheet, brightness plan and special cues on the concept sheet and take it to the meeting.
On the job tomorrow
  1. Find every cue at 100% brightness in the song you are rehearsing today.
  2. Pick one 100% in a section that isn’t energy 5, lower it to 85%, and see whether the peak looks bigger.
Closing Part 2

In Part 2 you decided what to show on stage to fit the song. You divided your equipment into roles (Chapter 6), read the concept from the music and agreed on it with the team (Chapter 7). You cut color down to two per scene (Chapter 8), set one main for each scene (Chapter 9), and decided when to save and when to bring out (Chapter 10).

In Part 3, “Directing the Song”, you place those decisions on the beats and bars of the song. It covers how fast to change brightness, color and movement, what to do in each section, how to layer the lights and how to pass from scene to scene. At the end you pull it all together on one cue sheet.
