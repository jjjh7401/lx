Chapter 3

Song Structure

From Intro to Outro

In this chapter
  • Tell the section names of pop and EDM apart, and know what the lights do in each section.
  • Give the same chorus a different intensity depending on which chorus it is.
  • Find sections that don’t land neatly on 8 bars and sections that come in one beat early.
Opening scene

A chorus usually appears two or three times in a song. The lyrics and melody are the same, yet the last chorus sounds unusually big. An instrument is added, the sound is emptied out just before it, or it hits one beat early.

If the lights treat all three choruses the same, they miss what the music prepared for the last one. So when you divide a song into sections, write the number along with the name. In this chapter, on top of the bars counted in Chapter 2, you mark the sections of the song one by one, and look for spots where a section doesn’t land neatly on the bars.

3.1 Section Names in Pop and EDM

A song is divided into a few sections, such as intro and chorus. For the same spot, song-centered pop and beat-centered EDM use different names.

The names used in pop and K-pop are usually these.

Terms to know — pop sections
Intro
The part that opens the song. Few instruments and low energy.
Verse
The part that tells the story. The melody stays the same while the lyrics change and repeat.
Pre-chorus
The part that builds anticipation before the chorus.
Chorus
The center of the song. The hook people sing along to is usually here.
Bridge
The part that goes somewhere different once after the chorus has repeated a few times.
Outro
The part that wraps up the song.

Academic researchers of musical structure use almost the same names when they label sections of a song.1 Moody said that most commercial pop has a "hook" that people remember and sing along to, and that spot is usually the chorus. The bigger the hook, the bigger the lighting should be.2

EDM calls its sections by other names, because it drives a song by stacking beats and layering sounds rather than by singing.

Terms to know — EDM sections
Build-up
Energy and tension rise little by little. The drums break into smaller pieces and the sound climbs upward.
Drop
Where the energy is highest. The main rhythm and the bass come in all at once.
Breakdown
Energy is set down and the song catches its breath. Often only the melody is left.

4,7

The two naming systems pair up. Chorus and drop are where the song bursts out the most, pre-chorus and build-up are where anticipation is built, and bridge and breakdown are where the song catches its breath.

Today’s K-pop often mixes both structures within one song. An EDM-style build-up comes before the chorus, and in the bridge’s place there is a drop-like section with no singing, only the beat. LOVE ATTACK has both. But whether to call that beat section a drop is not yet decided (Section 3.6).

What the lights do in each section is also roughly settled. Table 3-1 gathers the values given in practice material as a starting point. The numbers differ a little from one source to the next, which means the directing styles are just as varied.

Table 3-1 Starting point for the lights by section Starting value · 3
Section Job Brightness Movement Strobe
Intro Opening the door 20~40% Still or very slow None
Verse Telling the story 25~50% Still or very slow None
Pre-chorus / build-up Building anticipation 40–55%, rising gradually Add slow movement and narrow it gradually None to minimal
Chorus / drop Bursting out 80–100%, briefly Short bursts or controlled sweeps Brief only
Bridge / breakdown Catching a breath 20~35% Still None
Outro Closing Slowly lowered One slow fade None

The brightness in the table is a range gathered from practice material, so it differs a little from source to source. The starting values by section used in this book are collected again in Table 12-1 in Chapter 12.

In the table, a burst is a movement where moving lights fire off in a short rush, and a sweep is a movement where a beam sweeps from one side to the other.

The strongest effects are not dragged out. Practice material recommends using such effects for 1–4 beats and a white flash for only 1–2 beats, then returning straight to a calm base scene.3 It is the same principle as thinning out hits in Chapter 2.

3.2 Chorus Build

Even for the same chorus, give the lighting a different intensity depending on which chorus it is.

In this book, "chorus build" means the rule of raising the intensity one step each time the chorus returns. It is a different thing from the section name "build-up," which builds anticipation before the chorus. A song usually has two or three choruses. If you write all three simply as "chorus," the lights become the same all three times, and from the second time on there is nothing new for the audience.

So write a section as name and number together: Chorus 1, Chorus 2, Chorus 3. If the last chorus is arranged differently from the earlier ones, add a mark as well (Chorus 3′). This book calls LOVE ATTACK’s last chorus Chorus 3.

Formula

The name sets the intent and the number sets the intensity.

With only the name, the three choruses become the same; with only the number, you forget what that section is for. You have to look at both together.

Even datasets used in music structure research sometimes have no numbers. Open the annotation files of the Harmonix Set, which labels the sections of 912 songs, and the first chorus and the third chorus are both written simply as "chorus."4 For the people who built the data, "what kind of section is it" mattered, and for a lighting director "which one is it" matters just as much. The lighting director has to add the numbers themselves.

What changes with the number is shown in Table 3-2. These are the rules this book uses.

Table 3-2 Chorus build — LOVE ATTACK example Starting value
Pass Brightness Means of marking the entry Note
Chorus 1 (bar 18) 80% Brightness hit Matched to the entry where the drums burst
Chorus 2 (bar 46) 90% Switch to a moving-light burst Raise the brightness and change the means
Chorus 3 (bar 67 beat 4) 100% Switch to a white flash The music comes in a beat early, so the cue comes early too

There are two rules.

  1. Brightness may be stacked up from pass to pass.
  2. Use only one of the other means per cue. These are the moving burst (moving lights firing in a short rush), the color snap (changing color at once without a fade), the white flash, the blackout, the strobe, and the beam hit (narrowing or gathering beams to mark a moment). On the next pass, don’t stack one more on top of the previous means; switch to a different one.

If you pile several means into one cue, none of them reads clearly. If you switch one at a time, the audience sees a "different chorus" every time.

3.3 Sections That Are Not 8 Bars

8 bars is the most common section length, but many sections are shorter or longer.

Counting section lengths in the Harmonix Set in bars gives Figure 3-2 (author’s calculation, assuming 4/4).

8 bars is the most common at 42.6%, but it is less than half. Next come 4 bars (12.3%), 16 bars (12.1%) and 12 bars (5.7%), and other lengths make up as much as 27.3% (author’s calculation).4 Looking at the median length (the middle value when lined up by size) for each section name, verses, choruses, bridges and so on are 8 bars, and only the intro is 6 bars.4

LOVE ATTACK is the same. The intro is 6 bars, the first verse is 7 bars, and the second verse is 8 bars. Choruses 1 and 2 are 15 bars plus a 1-bar kick-out, making blocks of 16 bars.

So cutting a song into 8-bar pieces and hanging cues on that soon goes out of line. In LOVE ATTACK it goes out of line from the second section (bar 7, where Verse 1 starts). Expect 8 bars as the default, but check the real boundaries by ear.

How to check
  1. In your listening notes, write the section start bars in order and calculate the difference from the number just before.
  2. For LOVE ATTACK this is 1 → 7 → 14 → 18 → 33 → 34 → 42 → 46, and the differences are 6, 7, 4, 15, 1, 8, 4.
  3. Where a number other than 8 or 16 appears, that is where this song’s character is.

3.4 Sections That Come One Beat Early

Most new sections start on the downbeat, but one in ten starts a beat early.

Chapter 2 said that cues are usually hung on the first beat of the bar, the downbeat. Counting in the Harmonix Set, in fact, 81.1% of section starts fall on the downbeat (author’s calculation). About 10% start on beat 4 of the previous bar, that is, one beat early, and about 9% start on other beats.4

It means that once in ten times the music comes a beat early. In the Harmonix Set the median number of sections in a song is 10,4 so you can assume that each song has about one such spot.

At spots like this, hang the cue at the moment the music actually bursts. If the vocal or drums come in a beat early, put the cue on that beat (beat 4 of the previous bar). Don’t move it to the downbeat to fit the grid. At the live show, count the beats in your head and press GO on that very beat 4 (Chapter 31). If a chorus cue lands even one beat late, the audience notices immediately.

A short experiment
  1. Listen to the moment just before the last chorus of a song you like.
  2. Compare whether the spot where the "boom" lands after the drum fill ends is the same as the spot where the vocal starts the first line of the chorus.
  3. If they differ, decide which one to hang the cue on.

3.5 Sections K-pop Adds

K-pop dance songs often add sections for performance on top of the basic pop structure.

One study analyzed the bridges of 100 K-pop dance songs since 2004. According to it, what comes after the bridge has changed with the times, and recently structures that emphasize performance, such as dance breaks and post-choruses, are increasing.5

There are four K-pop sections a lighting director should know.

  • Post-chorusAttached right after the chorus to carry on the chorus’s energy. Often a repeated hook or beat comes to the front instead of the vocal. A safe way to start is to keep the chorus’s colors and lower only the intensity by one step (Starting value).
  • Dance breakA section where the singing stops and only dance fills it. It often comes in the bridge’s place after the second chorus, that is, right before the last chorus (Starting value; it varies by song). The rhythm can change or drop into half-time, so check "the tempo the body feels" from Chapter 2 again.
  • Kick-out barAt the end of a chorus the kick is removed for a bar or two to take a short rest. In LOVE ATTACK this is bars 33 and 61, and the kick also drops out at bar 82 at the end of the song. If the lights also lower the brightness for that one bar, the next section comes alive more sharply (Starting value).
  • Last chorus that comes in earlyThe last chorus is made special by changing how it enters compared with the first two. It is a place to be especially careful about the "one beat early" of Section 3.4. LOVE ATTACK’s Chorus 3 is like this.

Two things about this song are still undecided. One is what to call bars 63–66. This book writes it as "beat section (drop candidate)."

The other is whether the "beat drop" that critics talk about falls at bars 63–66 or at the chorus entries, bars 18 and 46. By measurement bars 63–66 are the likelier, but you have to listen and check for yourself. Chapter 5 covers how to leave such "undecided things" in a song map.

Key points

  • Pop uses intro, verse, pre-chorus, chorus and bridge; EDM uses build-up, drop and breakdown. Chorus and drop, pre-chorus and build-up, and bridge and breakdown are the pairs.
  • Write sections with a name and a number. The name sets the intent and the number sets the intensity.
  • As the pass number rises, stack the brightness and switch the other means, one per cue.
  • 8 bars is the most common but less than half. Check the boundaries by ear.
  • One section in ten starts a beat early. Hang the cue at the moment the music actually bursts.
  • In K-pop, watch for the post-chorus, dance break, kick-out bar and the last chorus that comes in early.
