Chapter 21

Festivals and Guest LDs

Lighting without knowing the set list

In this chapter
  • Build a busking page that lets you run a stage where you don’t know the songs in advance, as if playing an instrument by hand.
  • Tie the effects to a single BPM so that you can match the beat even to a song you are hearing for the first time.
  • Take in a stage that someone else designed in a short time, and match it to the video colors.
Opening scene

Picture the main stage of a summer festival. From dusk to midnight, one artist after another steps up, and every time the act changes, the rig on stage stays the same while the mood is completely different. The DJ watches how the crowd responds that day to choose the next track, and the band changes its set list just before the show.

The lighting of this stage starts without knowing what the song is. If in Parts 1–3 you read the song in advance and built a cue sheet, this chapter is the opposite. It covers how to stage what you hear as you hear it on a stage with no cue sheet, or one where the cue sheet does not go as written.

21.1 Lighting You Choose on the Spot — Busking

On a stage where you don’t know the set list, instead of a cue list you build the show out of buttons that you pick by hand as you go. This is called busking.

Just as a street musician picks a song on the spot, the lighting director picks and stacks color, position and effects on the spot.

Chris Kuroda, the lighting director of the American band Phish, shows the extreme of this approach. No performance is the same twice and there is no set list, so he said he runs 99% of the show on the spot. Because he cannot predict the first song, he said he prepares one “safety look” for it. When an improvisation stretches out, he also said he “plays the lights like an instrument.”1

There are two principles to take from this.

  • The safety lookKeep one basic look that is never wrong whatever comes. It has a base color and a wide wash, and no effects. The key is always on, on a fixed fader outside the page. It is where you return for the first song, when you lose the song, and during talk.
  • Playing with buttonsInstead of putting a whole scene into one cue, you press color, position, effect and accent separately and combine them. It is the sequences divided by role in Chapter 13, spread out so that you can play them by hand.

21.2 The Busking Page

In busking you line buttons up on one screen of the console (a page) and always use the same layout so that your hand can memorize the places. The starting value is to put only one kind of button in a row, and to place them so that they get stronger toward the right.

Table 21-1 The rows of a busking page Starting value 2 [Author to confirm]
Row Buttons it holds Left → right
Color Main + sub color combinations Cool colors → warm colors → white
Position Position presets (Chapter 9) Narrow (downlight, cross) → wide (fan, sky beam)
Dimmer FX Pulse, chase Slow (4-beat cycle) → fast (1-beat cycle)
Moving FX Pan/tilt waves, circle Small and slow → large and fast
Accents White flash, moving burst, strobe, audience Weak → strong
Safety Safety look, dark cue, blackout, freeze, white reset Always in the same place (the key is outside this page)

In this layout your hand moves from left to right, following the music. In a build-up (the part that heats up little by little), move across the dimmer effect buttons to the right and make it faster and faster. Just before a drop (the moment the music bursts all at once), press the dark cue, and at the drop press a wide position and one accent together (build-up and drop, Chapter 12). It is the order of Chapters 12 and 14, done by hand without a cue sheet.

21.3 Tying the Effects to One BPM

To match the beat of effects to a song you hear for the first time, tie the cycle of every effect to a single speed master (Chapter 2). Then when the song changes you only have to set the BPM again.

You set the BPM with tap tempo (a function where you tap a button on the beat to tell the console the speed). When the BPM is right, pulses, chases and waves all follow the beat of that song together. If you have set each effect’s cycle by its number of beats (Table 12-2 in Chapter 12), then even when the BPM changes, a “2-beat cycle” stays 2 beats.

  • Tap on the kickTap the tempo at least four times on the kick drum. If you tap on the hi-hat you get a value twice as fast.
  • Half-time and double-timeFor a song that feels half as fast (half-time in hip-hop) or a very fast song (160 BPM or more), read the BPM as half, and read a very slow song as double, and pick the tempo band of Chapter 12.
  • A default effect set for festivalsAssuming that many dance-stage songs fall within 120–130 BPM (Starting value), if you build in advance an effect set that looks good at 128 BPM (dimmer 1–2 beats, color 2–4 beats, position 4–8 beats), the start of the first song is faster (Starting value).
A short experiment
  1. Play a dance song you like and tap on the kick with the tap tempo function of a metronome (the beat calculator in the web tools works too).
  2. Compare how much the BPM differs from when you tap on the hi-hat.

21.4 A Stage You Did Not Design

When you step onto a stage someone else designed, first re-divide its equipment by role.

A festival’s guest LD (invited lighting designer) has to build a show on a stage another designer designed, and doesn’t have much time to prepare. Often you have to finish preparing not in a rehearsal slot of your own but while the act before you is performing, or in a few hours the night before. So when you receive the stage’s equipment list, you move it onto the fixture role table of Chapter 6. Then you first check which fixtures hold which role among key, back, side, beam, pixel, FX and audience, and whether any role is empty.

The side that designs the stage also builds the rig with guest LDs in mind. The team that designed the main stage of Ultra Miami in 2025 cut the number of fixture types to five. In the same article the designer named cloning, which copies the programming of one fixture to another on the console, as the function guest LDs lean on most.3 With fewer types, even a first-time LD quickly knows what each fixture does, and cloning becomes easier too.

21.5 Matching the Video Colors

A festival stage usually has a large LED video behind it, and the colors on screen decide the color of the whole stage. If the video is red and the lights are blue, the two fight.

So you agree on the main color of each song with the video team in advance, or if there is no time to agree, you look at the color of the video and pick the same or a neighboring color on the lighting’s color row.

If the video already fills the stage brightly, the lighting focuses on what the video cannot do: beams in haze (drawing lines in the air), the audience lights that light the seats, and the key that lights the artist. If the lighting does the same job as the video, the stage only gets brighter.

21.6 [Exercise] Build a Busking Page from the Chapter 6 Practice Rig

Imagine the practice rig of Chapter 6 (96 fixtures) is the rig of a stage someone else designed, and build one busking page. Then imagine LOVE ATTACK is a song you are hearing for the first time and try playing it with that page alone.

Follow along

  1. Group the fixtures by role and make groups in the order of Table 21-2.
  2. Put five or six two-color combinations on the color row. It does not matter if LOVE ATTACK’s palette (pearl pink, lilac, aqua) is not among them, because you assume it is a song you are hearing for the first time.
  3. Put about five on the position row, from narrow to wide.
  4. Make the dimmer effects and moving effects on a 128 BPM basis and tie them to the speed master.
  5. Fill the accent row and the safety row.
  6. Play LOVE ATTACK from the start, set the BPM with tap tempo, and play it with the page alone. Record, or write down, what you pressed whenever the section changed.
How to check
  1. After the performance, put what you pressed next to the cue sheet of Part 3 (Chapter 16).
  2. See how many of the dark cues in the cue sheet (bars 17, 45 and 67) you caught in the performance, whether you pressed the same buttons in every chorus (repetition inside a song), and whether there is any place where you pressed two or more accents at once.
Try it with your own song
  1. Build the same page with the rig of the show you are working on now, and practice with one song you have never heard, in the break between performances.
  2. Keep the same layout until your hand memorizes the places on the page.

Key points

  • On a stage where you don’t know the set list, run the show by busking: pick and stack buttons by hand instead of using a cue list.
  • Keep one safety look that is never wrong whatever comes, always in the same place. Keep the key outside the page.
  • On a busking page, put one kind of button in each row, getting stronger from left to right.
  • Tie every effect to the speed master, and tap the tempo on the kick.
  • On a stage someone else designed, first re-divide the fixtures by role. The side that designs the stage helps guest LDs by cutting down the types of fixtures.
  • When video fills the stage, the lighting focuses on what the video cannot do (beams, audience, key).
On the job tomorrow
  1. On the console you use now, check whether the safety look, dark cue, blackout and freeze are always in the same place.
  2. If not, make them in one row today.
