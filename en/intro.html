Introduction

Lighting That Listens

Watching a show with the sound off

Play a concert video with the sound muted and you can see when the lights change. What you can't easily tell is why they change at that exact moment.

Turn the sound back on and it all makes sense. The drums kick in hard, the singer takes a breath, every instrument hits at once as the chorus starts. The lights were changing on exactly those moments.

Good lighting looks a little flat with the sound off and feels inevitable with the sound on. This book is about how to make lighting that feels that inevitable.

What this book covers

This book walks through the whole process of lighting to music, in order, from start to finish.

  1. Listen to the song and note what changes, and where.
  2. Put those notes on a single sheet, in the order the song plays.
  3. Use that sheet to decide color, intensity and movement.
  4. Build it on the console (grandMA3 in this book), then check it in rehearsal and in the show.

It also covers what changes from one kind of stage to another: TV music shows, concerts, award shows, light shows and festivals. It is written to be easy to read for anyone who lights to music, from people just starting out to lighting directors and programmers with years on the job.

A book made with AI

I want to be upfront about one thing first. This book was written by AI. Everything from finding sources to organizing them, drafting the text and revising it was done with AI. My part was deciding what to cover, checking that the language matches what people actually say on the job, and pointing out what needed fixing.

It didn't start as a book. I am building an AI copilot that helps with lighting to music. For an AI to help with lighting, it first has to learn lighting direction: how to listen to a song, what rules turn what you hear into light, and how to build that on a console. So I gathered and organized a large body of material, including interviews and classes by lighting designers, console manuals, broadcast and safety regulations, and publicly available show footage.

Along the way it became clear that this material was useful to people, not just to an AI. So I put it together as a book and am sharing it with people who work in lighting.

Because it was written by AI, there may be mistakes. Where content has a source, the source is listed at the end of the chapter. If something differs from how you do it on the job, your experience is the better guide. Let me know and I will keep correcting it.

When to change the lights

The first question you face when lighting to music is: when do I change the look?

If you only change when the song changes in a big way, at the verse and the chorus, the stage stays the same for the whole verse. The music is moving but the stage looks frozen. If that bothers you and you change on every drum hit instead, the stage gets so busy that it buries the music.

The answer is somewhere in between. Changing often isn't the point. What matters is when you change, and how you carry the new look forward.

The requests you get on the job are always similar, too.

Things you often hear on the job
  • "Just make it look nice."
  • "The faces are washed out."
  • "Make it exactly like last week."
  • "Something should really pop here."

There's no single right answer to these, but there are ways to answer them. Find what "nice" means in the song and write it down in one line (Chapter 7). Leave the moment right before the "pop" briefly dark (Chapter 11). Keep the light on the faces in its own group so it stays steady no matter what effects are running (Chapter 13). This book collects ways to find answers like these.

It also includes many mistakes other people have made. It hurts less to learn from someone else's mistake first. They appear in each chapter as "Before / After" boxes and are collected again in Appendix F.

The most important idea in this book

If you remember only one thing, make it this. Intensity, direction and color each change at a different speed.

The eye notices intensity fastest, so change intensity with short, fast sounds like the drums. A moving light takes time to turn, so change its direction more slowly. Color takes the eye longest to settle into, so change it gradually over several bars, or all at once when the mood of the song shifts in a big way.

Remember just this and you will avoid changing the lights too rarely or too often. Chapters 1 to 3 explain step by step how to count music in beats and bars, and Chapter 11 covers this idea in detail.

Reference material you look up as needed is not part of the chapters. It is collected outside the book in the resource library. There you can search 153 effects by name, by song section or by sound. Every card has a moving preview, and some come with a macro you can load straight into the console. The library also holds work templates and reference tables, 49 fixture types, lighting design material and a set of grandMA3 commands. The appendix explains what is where.

The practice song and the web tools

Chapters 1 to 32 follow a single song as a running example: "LOVE ATTACK" by RESCENE. Think of it as the sample file in a textbook. In "Try it with your song" at the end of each chapter, you take the song you're working on through the same steps.

Throughout the book you'll find web tools: small tools that run right in your browser. What you write in your listening notes carries straight through to the song map, the cue sheet and the timecode table. You open your own music files. The book and the tools contain no audio, and the files you open never leave your browser.

Marks next to numbers

This book has a lot of numbers. The mark next to a number tells you where it came from.

  • Calculated: a value from a formula. Under the same conditions, anyone gets the same result.
  • Starting value: a value to try first. Adjust it to suit the job.
  • Superscript numbers: the source is listed at the end of the chapter.

Starting values aren't right answers. The same song plays differently on a different stage, and the same stage becomes a different show under a different LD. But once you know the defaults, you can also see when to break them.

One last thing

Lighting directors aren't musicians. Still, we're among the people who listen to the music the longest during a show. We hear the same song twenty times in rehearsal, and in the show we count silently, waiting for the moment to press the button.

I hope that after reading this book, when you listen to music, the places where the light should change come through a little more clearly. And I hope it's a small starting point for thinking, together with AI, about how to make better shows.
