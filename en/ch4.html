Chapter 4

Energy and Change

When a Song Swells and Settles

In this chapter
  • Divide a song’s energy into five levels, 1 to 5, and write them over the bars.
  • Find the biggest moment in the song and save the lights for it.
  • Write a song’s changes as points, impacts and transitions in symbols, and listen to the rhythm split into layers.
Opening scene

Think back to the times a show gave you goosebumps, and it was usually a moment when the sound suddenly changed. The sound that was packed full drops out for an instant and only a voice is left, and then drums and bass pour back into that empty space.

Moments like this are where the song’s energy changes. We divided the song into sections in Chapter 3, so now we listen for how full each section is and when and how that strength changes. The brightness and movement of the lights follow this change too.

4.1 Five Levels of Energy

This book calls how full and strong a song sounds its energy, and writes it in five levels, 1 to 5. Don’t look at loudness alone; decide by listening to the number of instruments, the weight of the bass and the density of the rhythm together.

Table 4-1 Cues and lighting by energy level Starting value
Level What you hear Common spot Lighting starting point
1 Sound almost empty. A voice or a single instrument Very start of the intro, the break right before a hit Lower the brightness but not to 0. Keep the people and the stage visible
2 The rhythm has started but is light. Weak bass Intro, early verse, kick-out bar One or two colors, movement still or very slow
3 Rhythm and bass are both there Verse, early pre-chorus Normal brightness, slow movement starts
4 Packed full. Many instruments and strong bass Chorus, drop Bright, movement matched to the beat
5 The biggest moment of the song Chorus/drop entry, last chorus The biggest means, one only, briefly
  • Energy 1 is still not lighting 0. If you switch the whole stage off because the intro is quiet, the audience sees only darkness through the first part of the song. 1 means "the smallest lighting," not "no lighting." The starting value for intro brightness in Table 3-1 of Chapter 3 is also 20–40%.
  • Put 5 only a few times in a song. If you give 5 to every chorus, it is no longer a 5 but a 4. Section 4.3 returns to this.
A short experiment
  1. Play any song and show 1–5 with your fingers each time the section changes.
  2. When the song ends, count how many times you held up a 5.
  3. If it was more than three times, raise your standard for 5 a little.

4.2 Energy a Level Meter Can’t Measure

Energy is not decided by the level-meter number alone. Measure LOVE ATTACK bar by bar and you see why.

The blue line is loudness per bar. The smallest bar (the intro) is 0.61 and the largest bar is 1.11. The red line is the strength of the low sounds of percussion such as the kick (35–120 Hz), in short, the percussion bass. The smallest bar is 0.23 and the largest bar is 4.43.1

The two lines are measured differently, so the numbers can’t be compared directly. Converted to the same unit (decibels), the range of loudness is about 5 dB and the range of the percussion bass is about 13 dB (Calculation). The range of the bass is more than double.

The change in loudness is clearly audible too. At bar 7, where the intro passes into the verse, the loudness rises sharply. But over the whole song, the swing of the bass is far bigger.

Listening, this song’s chorus entries clearly "burst." That moment comes less from overall loudness than from the kick and bass coming in and going out. Every song is different, so decide energy not from one number but by listening to several cues together.

  • BassWhether there is a kick and bass, and how full it is
  • Number of instrumentsHow many sounds ring at the same time
  • Density of the rhythmWhether it moves every beat, every half beat, or breaks into even smaller pieces
  • VoiceWhether it is alone, layered, or absent altogether
How to check
  1. After writing the energy, listen again to see whether the bass is really full in the sections you gave 4 and 5.
  2. If the bass is empty and you gave 4 or more, the verdict may have been swayed by the voice or the loudness.

4.3 The Biggest Moment

The moment a song bursts the most does not always come at the end, and it doesn’t come only once.

In LOVE ATTACK, the places where the percussion bass jumps the most are the entries of Chorus 1 and Chorus 2 (bars 18 and 46, about 4.4 times the song median). The entry bar of Chorus 3 (bar 68) comes out at an average of 2.5 times.

That is not because Chorus 3 is small. Chorus 3’s hit comes a beat early and starts on bar 67 beat 4, so when you average per bar, the hit’s bass is split across two bars and comes out low.1 It means you can’t compare the three chorus entries fairly with bar averages.

Then look at what is different about Chorus 3. By measurement, the body of Chorus 3 (bars 69–81) has loudness and bass at about the same level as the bodies of Choruses 1 and 2.

What is different is how it comes in. The sound is stripped back just before (bar 67), it strikes in a beat early, then rises once more at bars 69–70 and carries on to the end of the song. What makes Chorus 3 special is not size but contrast and timing.

In this verdict, 5 goes in only three places: the first bar of Choruses 1 and 2, and bar 67 beat 4 and bar 68, where Chorus 3 comes in early. The bodies of all the choruses are 4. Bar 67 sounds the emptiest, so I judged it 1. By the measured values alone, bar 67 is louder than the intro and a hit is mixed into beat 4. So this 1 is a verdict made by ear, not by measurement, and in Chapter 5’s notation it is ◐.

The shape of how energy flows differs from song to song. Figure 4-3 shows three shapes you often see.

Whichever shape it is, the job for the lights is the same. Keep something back for the biggest moment. Moody wrote that you must save something for the end so that the song doesn’t finish flat.2 If you use up the blinders and white flashes early, you have nothing to bring out in Chorus 3. The chorus build in Table 3-2 of Chapter 3 does exactly this job.

4.4 Points, Impacts and Transitions

If energy is the size of a section, change is the spot where that size changes. This book divides changes into three kinds by length and strength.

  • PointA single spot on one beat where something comes in or changes. This includes the entry of a new instrument (▲) and the anticipation (◆, striking early) where the next section comes in a beat early.
  • ImpactThe spot among the points that bursts the most. The big hit or drop (▼) where bass and drums pour in all at once is typical, and the spot where you give energy 5 is usually an impact.
  • TransitionA stretch of a few beats to a few bars that carries over into the next thing. This includes the build-up (↗), snare roll (≋), break (○) and kick-out (×).

In listening notes and the song map, these three are written as symbols. Points and impacts are written as a bar and a beat, and transitions as from which bar to which bar.

The transition that appears most often is the build-up. The musicologist Solberg analyzed build-ups and drops in EDM and named five production techniques that create tension and anticipation.3

  1. Laying down a rising sound effect (an uplifter)
  2. Rushing the drums into smaller and smaller pieces (a drum roll)
  3. Greatly changing the band between high and low sounds
  4. Removing the bass and kick and then putting them back
  5. Placing a contrasting breakdown right before

Translated to lighting, it goes like this (Starting value).

  • During the build-up, raise brightness, speed and zoom together a little at a time, but narrow the range of movement instead. Save the wide spread for the spot where it will burst.
  • Use dense beats only in the last 1–2 bars where the drum roll rushes.
  • If the bass drops out or the sound empties right before a hit, take the lights down one step too, or go dark briefly. Only with darkness does the next light look big.
  • At the hit, use one of the biggest means (Section 3.2 in Chapter 3).

Bars 42–45 are a good example. Solberg’s "removing the bass and kick and then putting them back" is right there. If the lights also go down one step in bars 42–43 and come up in bars 44–45, the hit at bar 46 looks much bigger.

4.5 Rhythm Layers

Listen to the rhythm up close and the sounds of several instruments are piled in layers. These layers are called layers.

Divide a bar into 16 cells (sixteenth notes, a quarter of a beat) and you can see where each layer makes its sound.

  • Pop backbeatThe snare lands on 2 and 4. This is the cue used in Chapter 2 to check the BPM by ear.
  • Four on the floorThe kick lands on every beat. The basis of house and a lot of EDM.
  • Half-time and sub bass (808)The snare lands only on beat 3, with a sub bass laid underneath. The name in parentheses comes from Roland’s 1980 drum machine, the TR-808, and now refers to a heavy low sound that rings for a long time (something between a kick and a bass).4 In this book I write "sub bass (808)" the first time and shorten it to "sub bass" after that.

Once you listen by layers, you can divide the lights into layers too. The kick pairs with a brightness pulse, the snare and clap with a short flash, the hi-hat with a finely flickering texture, and the sub bass or bass with the brightness floor of a wash (Figure 1-2 in Chapter 1). If you give each layer to a different group of lights, you avoid one group getting busy following every sound. This method is covered in detail in Chapter 13, "Layering."

A short experiment
  1. Play one chorus bar on repeat, and this time tap the table with your finger following only the hi-hat.
  2. Count how many times you tapped in one bar.
  3. 8 means eighth-note hi-hats and 16 means sixteenth-note hi-hats.
  4. Check with Table 2-4 in Chapter 2 how many Hz you get if you flash the lights at that speed.

Key points

  • Write energy as 1–5. 1 is still not lighting 0, and 5 goes only a few times in a song.
  • Don’t decide energy from the level-meter number alone. Listen to the bass, the number of instruments, the density of the rhythm and the voice together.
  • The biggest moment may not be at the end of the song, and may not be only one. Keep one means back for that moment.
  • Write a song’s changes as symbols, divided into points (a single spot), impacts (the spot that bursts the most) and transitions (stretches that carry over). The break right before an impact is what makes the impact big.
  • If you listen to the rhythm split into layers, you can hand the lights out by layer too.
On the job tomorrow
  1. Count how many places you gave energy 5 in the song you are cueing today.
  2. Put a cue that takes the brightness down one step into the bar right before the biggest hit.
